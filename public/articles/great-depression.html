The Great Depression

The event that broke faith in self correcting markets and opened the door to Keynesian economics.

Econ Nexus Research Hub Briefing Topic: Banking Crises and Macroeconomic Policy Reading time: 17 minutes

The Wall Street crash of October 1929 wiped out enormous paper wealth in days, but it was the chain reaction that followed, bank failures, collapsing spending, and a global wave of protectionism, that turned a stock market panic into a decade long depression. This briefing walks through the output and unemployment data, the two rival explanations economists still debate, and the policy lessons drawn from it ever since.

1. Timeline: from crash to recovery

  • October 1929. The Wall Street crash wipes out a large share of stock market value in days.
  • 1930 to 1933. A wave of bank failures destroys household savings and sharply reduces the money supply.
  • 1930. The Smoot Hawley Tariff Act raises United States tariffs; other countries retaliate, and global trade contracts sharply.
  • 1933. President Franklin Roosevelt launches the New Deal, including deposit insurance and public works programs.
  • 1933 to 1936. Partial recovery, though unemployment remains historically high.
  • 1937. A premature tightening of fiscal and monetary policy triggers a sharp relapse.

4. A third explanation: the gold standard as a transmission belt

A body of research associated with economic historian Barry Eichengreen adds a mechanism the monetarist and Keynesian stories both underplay: the international gold standard itself. Under the interwar gold standard, countries fixed their currencies to gold, which meant a central bank fighting a domestic slump could not simply cut interest rates or expand the money supply without risking a gold outflow and a currency crisis. This tied the hands of policymakers almost everywhere at once, turning what might have been a national banking panic into a globally synchronized contraction.

The clearest evidence for this view is comparative: countries that abandoned the gold standard earlier recovered sooner. The United Kingdom left gold in 1931 and began recovering relatively quickly; the United States did not effectively leave until 1933; France and other members of the so called gold bloc clung to it until the mid 1930s and suffered correspondingly longer downturns. This pattern, where the timing of leaving gold predicts the timing of recovery almost country by country, is one of the more robust empirical findings in Depression era economic history, and it reframes the crisis as partly a story about a rigid international monetary system rather than purely a domestic policy failure in any one country.

5. The policy response

Roosevelt's New Deal introduced deposit insurance through the FDIC to prevent future bank runs, public works programs to directly employ idle labor, and the first federal unemployment insurance system. These institutions remain part of the standard toolkit for managing modern recessions, though the Depression itself was not resolved until wartime spending during the Second World War drove a much larger fiscal expansion than the New Deal alone had achieved.

6. A contested verdict: did the New Deal itself slow the recovery

A less commonly taught but influential strand of research complicates the standard pro New Deal narrative. Economists Harold Cole and Lee Ohanian, in a widely cited 2004 study published in the Journal of Political Economy, argued that certain New Deal measures actually prolonged the Depression rather than ending it. Their focus was the National Industrial Recovery Act, which suspended antitrust law and let industries set codes of fair competition, in effect permitting price fixing and cartel behavior provided firms raised wages and recognized collective bargaining.

Cole and Ohanian's modeling attributes roughly 60 percent of the persistent gap between actual output and the economy's normal trend growth after 1933 to these cartelization and high wage policies, arguing that keeping wages above their market clearing level discouraged hiring even as monetary and banking conditions improved. This finding remains genuinely contested among economic historians, and it does not question the value of deposit insurance or public works spending, only the specific NIRA style codes, which the Supreme Court in fact struck down as unconstitutional in 1935. It is a useful example for essays: the Depression is not a settled morality tale with the New Deal simply as hero, but an ongoing empirical argument where different named studies support different, sometimes contradictory, policy lessons.

7. Beyond America: how the Depression became a global and political crisis

The domestic American story, crash, bank runs, tariff, New Deal, is only half the picture, and essays that stop at the US border miss the scale of what happened. Roughly nine thousand American banks failed between 1930 and 1933, wiping out depositors' savings with no insurance to protect them, which is precisely the gap deposit insurance was created to close afterward. The US money supply contracted by roughly a third over the same period, a monetary collapse of a size almost never seen since in a developed economy.

The crisis hit Germany especially hard because German banks and businesses were heavily dependent on short term American loans that were suddenly recalled after the crash, triggering a German banking crisis in 1931 on top of an economy still scarred by the hyperinflation of 1923. Mass unemployment in Germany during the early 1930s is one of the standard factors historians cite in explaining the collapse of support for the Weimar Republic and the rise of the Nazi party, which took power in January 1933, making the Depression not only an economic event but arguably the single most consequential political trigger of the twentieth century.

8. The 1937 relapse

In 1937, the Federal Reserve tightened monetary policy and the federal government cut spending, both partly in response to signs of recovery. The result was a sharp new recession, unemployment climbing back toward 19 percent, widely cited afterward as a lesson that withdrawing stimulus too early can undo a fragile recovery.

9. Why it still matters

The Depression is the standard case study for essays on business cycles, banking crises, monetary policy failure, and protectionism. It is also the origin point for modern policy tools such as deposit insurance and unemployment insurance, and it is routinely compared with the 2008 financial crisis and the COVID nineteen shock in essays evaluating how policy responses have evolved.

10. Key takeaways

  1. The Depression supports two rival policy lessons at once, a monetarist story about a mismanaged money supply and a Keynesian story about deficient demand.
  2. Protectionism made the downturn worse, a lesson still cited in modern debates on tariffs.
  3. Withdrawing stimulus too early can trigger a relapse, as the 1937 episode demonstrates.

Glossary

Bank run
A situation where many depositors withdraw funds simultaneously, fearing a bank's collapse, which can itself cause the collapse.
Deflation
A sustained fall in the general price level, which raises the real burden of debt.
New Deal
Franklin Roosevelt's set of programs and reforms launched from 1933, including deposit insurance and public works employment.
Smoot Hawley Tariff
A 1930 US law raising tariffs on many imports, widely cited as worsening the global economic contraction through retaliation.
Gold standard
A monetary system fixing a currency's value to a set amount of gold, which limited central banks' ability to expand money supply during the Depression without risking a gold outflow.
National Industrial Recovery Act (NIRA)
A 1933 law suspending antitrust enforcement and allowing industry codes of fair competition; struck down by the Supreme Court in 1935 and later blamed by some economists for slowing recovery.
Money supply contraction
The roughly one-third fall in the US money supply between 1929 and 1933 as thousands of banks failed, central to the monetarist explanation of the Depression's severity.
