JPMorgan, the World's Banking Giants, and AI: A Complete Guide

How one bank's balance sheet moves global markets, how commercial banks quietly create most of the money in the economy, why the biggest banks almost never compete on price, and how JPMorgan, Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley, and Bank of America are actually deploying artificial intelligence right now, in plain, accurate English, with A-Level-standard diagrams built up slowly, stage by stage.

When people talk about "the economy," they usually picture governments, central banks, and companies. They forget the institution sitting underneath all three: the commercial bank. Every pound or dollar you own is really just an entry on a bank's books, every mortgage, every business loan, and every wire transfer between countries passes through a small number of enormous banks, and JPMorgan Chase sits at the very centre of that system, officially ranked the world's single most systemically important bank. This guide explains how that system actually works, using real A-Level economics diagrams built from real numbers rather than sketched by eye, and connects each one to what JPMorgan and its closest rivals are doing with AI right now, not as a future prediction, but as it stands in 2026.

1. Who actually runs the global banking system

JPMorgan Chase is not just a large bank, it is officially the largest and most important bank on Earth, by two different, very deliberate measures. First, by size: with roughly $3.2 trillion in assets and a market value crossing $830 billion, JPMorgan retained the top spot on Forbes' Global 2000 ranking of the world's largest public companies for the fourth year running in 2026. Second, and more importantly, by systemic weight: the Financial Stability Board's annual list of Global Systemically Important Banks (G-SIBs), the official ranking regulators use to decide which banks would do the most damage to the world economy if they failed, has placed JPMorgan in its highest risk category, "bucket 4," every year since 2021, the only bank in the world currently in that category. That single fact forces JPMorgan to hold an extra 2.5% capital buffer above every other bank on the planet, a direct regulatory acknowledgement that if JPMorgan wobbles, the whole system wobbles with it.

JPMorgan and its closest peers, Bank of America, Citigroup, Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley, Wells Fargo, and internationally HSBC and China's four giant state banks, influence the world economy through channels most people never see directly: they are the primary dealers who buy government debt directly from central banks and resell it to the market, the market-makers who quote prices in almost every currency pair traded globally, the underwriters who decide which companies get to raise capital and on what terms, and, as the next section explains, the actual creators of most of the money supply itself.

2. Where banks sit in the circular flow of income, precisely drawn

Before going further, it helps to place JPMorgan on the simplest possible map of the whole economy: the circular flow of income. Households and firms exchange income for output in a closed loop, and sitting between them is the financial sector, banks like JPMorgan, which channels household saving out of the loop and firm investment back into it. Everything in the rest of this article, credit creation, interest rates, and the foreign exchange market, is really just a close-up of what happens inside that one box.

Every dollar of household saving (S) that flows into JPMorgan is a potential deposit that the credit-creation process can multiply up to ten times over, which is precisely why the size and behaviour of a handful of banks like JPMorgan can move the entire economy's money supply.

3. How commercial banks create money, precisely drawn

Here is the idea that surprises almost every student the first time they meet it properly: banks do not simply lend out money that savers have deposited with them. When JPMorgan approves a loan, it does not search its vaults for spare cash, it creates a brand new deposit in the borrower's account, out of nothing, on the spot. That new deposit is new money. It did not exist a second before the loan was approved. This is not a conspiracy theory, it is standard central bank doctrine: the Bank of England has stated explicitly that the principal way money is created in a modern economy is through commercial banks making loans.

What stops banks creating infinite money is the reserve requirement: regulators require banks to hold back a fraction of every deposit as reserves, rather than lending all of it out. The diagram below shows exactly how one deposit at JPMorgan cascades through the banking system, shrinking a little at each step, but adding up to a total money supply many times larger than where it started.

Figure 1. JPMorgan receives a $1,000 deposit, keeps the required 10% reserve, and lends the rest onward. That loan becomes a new deposit at the next bank, which repeats the process, keeping 10% and lending 90%, forever shrinking. Add up every round and the total money supply created converges on $10,000, exactly ten times the original deposit, because the money multiplier is the inverse of the reserve ratio, 1 ÷ 10% = 10.
Why this is accurate: with an initial deposit D₀=$1,000 and reserve ratio r=10%, each successive deposit is exactly 90% of the last: $1,000, $900, $810, $729…, a genuine geometric series. The cumulative total after n rounds is D₀·(1−0.9n)/(1−0.9), which is why the bars in the diagram are computed values, $1,000, $1,900, $2,710, $3,439, $4,095.10, $4,685.59, converging on the algebraic limit of $10,000, not an artist's impression of "banks lending more than one bank has."

4. The money market: how JPMorgan-sized banks move interest rates, precisely drawn

Interest rates, the single most important price in the entire economy, are set where the demand to hold money meets the supply of it. Economists call this the liquidity preference model, or simply the money market. The demand for money slopes downward because, as interest rates rise, the opportunity cost of holding non-interest-bearing cash rather than an interest-paying bond rises with it, so people and firms choose to hold less idle cash. The supply of money, at any given moment, is treated as fixed by the central bank and the banking system together, drawn as a vertical line.

MS0 (vertical at Q=30) and MS1 (vertical at Q=45) meet these curves at exactly the algebraic points r0=10%, r1=6.67%, and r2=5%, each solved directly from the equations, not estimated from the picture.

5. AI and monetary policy: faster money, a quieter multiplier

Figure 2's second shift is not hypothetical. JPMorgan has spent years building Kinexys (formerly Onyx), its blockchain-based wholesale payments platform, which already moves over $2 billion a day and allows corporate clients to settle transactions between accounts instantly, twenty-four hours a day, instead of waiting for next-day settlement. Every dollar that used to sit idle in a corporate account "just in case," waiting for a payment to clear, is exactly the kind of transactions demand for money that the MD curve in Figure 2 represents. The faster and more predictable payments become, the less idle cash firms need to hold, and the further left the MD curve shifts.

  • AI-driven cash forecasting. Large corporate treasuries increasingly use AI models to predict their cash needs hour by hour rather than day by day, letting them hold smaller cash buffers and shifting money demand left, exactly as in Figure 2's third stage.
  • A quieter multiplier, not a smaller one. AI does not change the mathematics of the credit multiplier in Figure 1, the reserve ratio still governs how much new money each loan can generate, but it does change the speed at which each round happens: an AI-assisted loan approval that once took days can now be underwritten in minutes, compressing the whole multiplier process into a much shorter window of time.
  • Central banks are watching closely. A money supply that can expand and contract faster because of AI-accelerated lending decisions is, in principle, a money supply that is harder for a central bank to steer smoothly, one of the newer risks regulators are actively studying.

6. From interest rates to the real economy: monetary transmission, precisely drawn

Figure 2 showed how AI-driven bank lending and AI-driven cash efficiency pull the interest rate down. That interest rate change does not stop inside the money market, it flows straight into the real economy: a lower interest rate makes borrowing cheaper for firms, so investment spending (I) rises, which is itself an injection into the circular flow from Figure 0. Aggregate demand shifts right, and, exactly as in the AD/AS model, that shows up as some mix of higher real output and higher prices.

7. JPMorgan and the foreign exchange market, precisely drawn

JPMorgan is not just a lender, it is also one of the handful of banks that make the market in almost every major currency pair traded globally, quoting the prices that determine what a dollar is worth against a pound, a euro, or a yen at any given moment. The foreign exchange market works exactly like any other market: an exchange rate is simply the price of one currency in terms of another, set where global supply and demand for that currency meet.

8. Why the giants rarely compete on price: the banking oligopoly, precisely drawn

Global banking looks like a competitive industry, thousands of banks worldwide, but the vast majority of investment banking fees, trading volume, and cross-border payments flow through a small handful of giants: JPMorgan, Bank of America, Citigroup, Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley, and a handful of international peers. This is a textbook oligopoly, a market dominated by a few large, interdependent firms, and oligopolies famously produce a very specific pattern: prices that barely move for years at a time, even while costs shift underneath them. The kinked demand curve model explains exactly why.

Four-stage build, watch closely
↔ swipe to see the full diagram, or rotate your phone
Figure 3. Above today's price P0, demand is elastic, rivals let a price rise happen and simply absorb the customers who leave, so the curve is flat. Below P0, demand is inelastic, rivals match every price cut immediately, so a bank gains almost no extra volume for a lower price, and the curve is steep. This kink creates a vertical break, or "gap," in the marginal revenue curve directly below it. As long as a bank's marginal cost curve passes through that gap, the profit-maximising price stays glued to P0, no matter how much costs actually move.
Why this is accurate: the upper (elastic) demand segment is the true line P = 87.5−0.75Q, giving marginal revenue MR = 87.5−1.5Q by the standard doubling rule, so MR at the kink (Q=50) is exactly 12.5. The lower (inelastic) segment, P = 125−1.5Q, gives MR = 125−3Q, which is already negative at Q=50, this sharp drop from +12.5 to a negative value is the genuine algebraic source of the MR gap, not an artistic exaggeration. MC0 (a constant $10) and MC1 (a constant $5, representing AI-driven cost savings) both fall inside that gap, which is precisely why the diagram can show two very different cost levels landing on the exact same price and quantity.

9. AI and the kinked demand curve: cutting costs without cutting prices

Figure 3's final stage describes something genuinely happening across the banking industry right now. AI has driven real, measurable cost savings inside the biggest banks, JPMorgan alone reports its AI tools have generated efficiency gains of 30% to 40% for staff using its LLM Suite platform, and roughly $2 billion a year in total savings from AI initiatives. Under normal competitive conditions, a cost saving that large would be expected to show up as lower fees for customers. In an oligopoly with a kinked demand curve, it usually does not, because no individual bank wants to be the one that breaks ranks and starts a fee war its rivals will simply match.

Where the competition actually happens instead

Instead of competing on price, the world's top banks are pouring their AI-driven savings into non-price competition, better research tools for financial advisors, faster fraud detection, more personalised digital assistants, exactly the pattern the kinked demand curve model predicts oligopolists will do when direct price competition is too costly to start. Bank of America's AI assistant Erica has handled more than 3.2 billion client interactions; Morgan Stanley built a dedicated AI research assistant directly with OpenAI for its 16,000 financial advisors; Goldman Sachs rolled its GS AI Assistant out firm-wide after reporting developer productivity gains north of 20%. Each bank is racing to out-serve its rivals with AI rather than to out-price them.

11. Too big to fail, too automated to trust?

JPMorgan's position at the top of the G-SIB list exists precisely because regulators learned, in 2008, what happens when a bank this interconnected gets into trouble, everyone else gets pulled down with it. AI adds a genuinely new dimension to that old worry. As more of the credit-creation process described in Figure 1, and more of the trading and risk decisions inside these institutions, become partly automated, regulators are increasingly asking a new version of the "too big to fail" question: what happens if the automated systems across several of the world's most interconnected banks make correlated mistakes at the same time, not because any single bank behaved recklessly, but because they were all trained on similar data and reacting to the same market signal?

  • Concentration risk. A small number of AI model providers (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google) now sit underneath the AI tools at JPMorgan, Goldman Sachs, and Morgan Stanley simultaneously, a new kind of interconnection regulators did not have to model in 2008.
  • Explainability requirements. Because JPMorgan sits in the strictest regulatory bucket, its own public statements on LLM Suite specifically emphasise explainability and data governance, a direct response to the expectation that a bank this systemically important cannot deploy AI decisions it cannot later explain to a regulator.
  • Jamie Dimon's own framing. Even the industry's most prominent AI advocate has called for governments and employers to share responsibility for supporting workers displaced by AI-driven automation, an unusually candid acknowledgement from the CEO of the world's most systemically important bank that the technology's benefits and risks will not be evenly distributed.

13. Key takeaways

  1. JPMorgan is not just the world's largest bank by several rankings, it is officially the single most systemically important bank on Earth, the only institution in the FSB's highest G-SIB risk category.
  2. Commercial banks create most of the money supply themselves, every loan is a new deposit, and the credit multiplier (1 ÷ reserve ratio) determines exactly how far a single deposit can expand through the banking system.
  3. Interest rates are set in the money market where money demand meets money supply, and both AI-driven bank lending and AI-driven cash efficiency are actively shifting both curves right now.
  4. The kinked demand curve explains why the biggest banks' fees stay rigid even when AI genuinely cuts their costs, the marginal revenue "gap" absorbs cost changes without moving the price.
  5. AI investment at the top of global banking is now measured in the tens of billions of dollars a year, and every major bank so far reports using it to augment staff and cut costs rather than to replace headcount at scale.
  6. AI introduces a new, still poorly understood form of systemic interconnection between the world's largest banks, through their shared reliance on a small number of AI model providers, precisely the kind of concentrated risk that G-SIB regulation was designed to catch.

14. Glossary

Global Systemically Important Bank (G-SIB)
A bank designated by the Financial Stability Board as posing significant risk to the global financial system if it were to fail, requiring extra capital buffers.
Credit creation
The process by which commercial banks create new money by issuing loans, which appear as new deposits rather than transfers of existing cash.
Reserve ratio
The fraction of each deposit a bank is required to hold back rather than lend out, which determines the size of the credit (money) multiplier.
Money multiplier
The ratio by which an initial deposit can expand the total money supply; calculated as 1 ÷ the reserve ratio.
Liquidity preference (money market) model
A model of interest rate determination where a downward-sloping demand for money meets a supply of money treated as fixed by the central bank and banking system.
Oligopoly
A market structure dominated by a small number of large, interdependent firms, where each firm's pricing decisions must account for how rivals will react.
Kinked demand curve
A model explaining price rigidity in oligopoly: demand is elastic above the current price (rivals ignore a rise) and inelastic below it (rivals match a cut), producing a discontinuous marginal revenue curve.
Marginal revenue (MR) gap
The vertical discontinuity in the marginal revenue curve directly beneath a kink in demand, within which a firm's marginal cost can shift without changing its profit-maximising price or output.
Primary dealer
A bank authorised to trade government securities directly with a central bank, giving it a privileged role in the transmission of monetary policy.
