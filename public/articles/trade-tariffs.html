America Has Been Arguing About Trade for 250 Years

Two long-run charts — trade as a share of GDP, and average tariff rates since 1790 — put the current tariff debate in a perspective that neither side of the argument usually bothers to supply.

Background essay based on research by Douglas A. Irwin Peterson Institute for International Economics, July 1, 2026 Topic: US trade policy since 1790

Trade policy debates tend to be conducted as though the current dispute — over tariffs, deficits, or which country is "winning" — is a novel crisis with no precedent. Douglas A. Irwin's brisk overview for the Peterson Institute for International Economics is a useful corrective: Americans have been arguing over trade for more than two and a half centuries, and the long-run data on both trade volumes and tariff rates tell a story considerably more textured than either side of today's debate usually acknowledges.

❦

Section OneTwo Eras of Trade as a Share of GDP

Looking at combined imports and exports as a share of GDP from 1790 to 2025 reveals two distinct regimes. From roughly 1840 to 1970 — a span of well over a century, encompassing the Civil War, industrialization, two world wars, and the early Cold War — US trade hovered remarkably consistently around 5 percent of GDP. Then, starting around 1970, trade's share of GDP began rising sharply, a rise that continued for roughly four decades before leveling off over the past ten to fifteen years.

That inflection point around 1970 marks a genuine break in how integrated the American economy is with the rest of the world — a shift usually attributed to falling transportation and communication costs, successive rounds of multilateral trade liberalization, and the rise of globally organized supply chains. The recent plateau, by contrast, suggests that integration may be approaching some kind of practical ceiling, or at least pausing, rather than continuing to rise indefinitely.

❦

Section TwoThe Deficit Is the More Recent Story

A second, less discussed pattern in the same data is arguably more consequential. For most of US history through the early 1990s, imports and exports were in rough balance, give or take occasional exceptions. For the past thirty years or so, that balance has broken down: imports have consistently exceeded exports.

For most of US history, imports and exports were in rough balance — for the last 30 years, imports have consistently exceeded exports.

Framed in national accounting terms, this means total US consumption — domestic production plus net imports — has been running ahead of total US production for three decades running. The politically convenient explanation blames trading partners for not buying enough American exports. But arithmetically, a persistent trade deficit is just as much a statement about the United States consuming more than it produces, which points the analysis back toward domestic saving and borrowing behavior — most visibly, the scale of the federal budget deficit — rather than exclusively toward the trade practices of other countries.

Section ThreeThe Long Arc of Tariff Rates

The second chart in Irwin's overview tracks average US tariff rates from 1790 to 2025, calculated two different ways: one line shows average tariffs applied only to the imports that are actually taxed, while a second shows average tariffs relative to all imports, including goods that enter tariff-free. The big-picture pattern is a long decline followed by a recent reversal: tariffs were considerably higher through much of the nineteenth century, dropped sharply in the wake of the Great Depression and the Second World War, and have risen again recently.

Reading the Gap Between the Two Lines

When the two tariff lines sit close together, as they did from 1790 through roughly 1870, it means tariffs were being applied broadly across most imports. As the lines diverge later in US history, it signals that tariffs were increasingly applied selectively — targeted at specific industries and goods rather than uniformly across all trade — which pushes the tariff rate on taxed goods notably higher than the tariff rate calculated against total imports.

❦

Section FourWhat Tariffs Used to Pay For

A final data point supplies useful context often missing from tariff debates: for roughly the first 130 years of US history, federal spending typically ran around 2 percent of GDP — a figure so low that tariff revenue alone could plausibly fund most of the federal government. The sequence of the First World War, the Great Depression, and the Second World War changed that permanently: federal spending has averaged about 21 percent of GDP over the past fifty years, funded overwhelmingly by individual and corporate income taxes along with payroll taxes. Tariffs, which were once a primary source of federal revenue, have not been a major funding mechanism for the federal government since roughly 1950 — meaning any modern case for tariffs has to rest on trade or industrial-policy grounds rather than on the government's need for revenue, which is a fundamentally different justification than the one that applied for most of the tariff's 250-year history.
