The 2026 Tariff Controversy: A Complete, Balanced Guide

How a Supreme Court ruling, a temporary 10% global tariff, and two competing forecasts for the U.S. economy are reshaping prices, investment, and the 2026 midterm political landscape — explained mechanism by mechanism, with both sides of the growth-versus-recession debate given equal weight.

EconNexus Deep Dive Topic: Trade Policy, Inflation, and GDP Forecasting Reading time: ~40 minutes · 4 interactive diagrams Sources: Yale Budget Lab, Goldman Sachs Research, Morgan Stanley, PIIE, Supreme Court opinions, agency disclosures

On February 20, 2026, the Supreme Court ended a year-long legal fight by ruling, 6–3, that the president does not have the power to impose sweeping tariffs under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act. Within hours, the administration had replaced them with a new 10% global tariff under a different law. Six months later, the tariffs are still here, the economic argument about what they're doing to the economy is louder than ever, and two of Wall Street's most respected research desks have reached genuinely different conclusions about where 2026 is headed. This guide walks through the mechanics, the law, and both sides of that argument, without picking one.

1. Historical context: a century of shifting tariff power

The U.S. Constitution gives Congress, not the president, the power "to lay and collect taxes, duties, imposts and excises." For America's first 150 years, that's exactly who set them: Congress passed the McKinley Tariff of 1890, pushing average duties on taxed imports to roughly 50%, and then, in 1930, the Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act, which raised duties on over 20,000 imported goods and is widely blamed, alongside the Federal Reserve's tight money policy, for deepening the Great Depression as trading partners retaliated and global trade collapsed.

Smoot-Hawley's damage pushed Congress in the opposite direction. The 1934 Reciprocal Trade Agreements Act began a decades-long trend of Congress delegating tariff-setting authority to the president, a trend that continued through the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade in 1947 and the creation of the World Trade Organization in 1995. By the 2010s, the average U.S. tariff on dutiable imports had fallen under 5%, among the lowest in American history. Trump's first term reversed course modestly, using Section 301 of the 1974 Trade Act to impose tariffs covering roughly $370 billion of Chinese imports and Section 232 of the 1962 Trade Expansion Act for steel and aluminum. Both authorities required formal investigations and took months to deploy.

What made 2025 different was the choice of legal vehicle. On April 2, 2025, a day the administration branded "Liberation Day," it imposed a 10% baseline tariff on virtually every country on Earth plus much larger "reciprocal" rates on major trading partners, not through Section 301 or 232, but through the International Emergency Economic Powers Act, a 1977 law never before used to impose tariffs at all, written to let presidents freeze assets and impose sanctions during declared national emergencies. IEEPA has no investigation requirement and no time limit, which is precisely why it enabled tariff rates to be announced by proclamation, changed by tweet, and stacked country by country in a way none of the older statutes allowed. That legal shortcut is also exactly what the Supreme Court found unlawful ten months later.

2. How tariffs mechanically work, precisely drawn

Strip away the politics and a tariff is a simple thing: a tax collected by U.S. Customs and Border Protection on goods as they cross the border, paid by the American importer of record, not by the foreign government or, directly, the foreign company. What makes tariffs economically interesting is what happens next, and the standard trade-theory diagram below shows exactly why economists across the political spectrum agree tariffs raise domestic prices and create real economic waste, even before anyone argues about how large that waste is.

The four regions are constructed geometrically from the same two straight-line supply and demand curves, area a's right edge, area b's hypotenuse, area c's left edge, and area d's hypotenuse are all literally the same S and D lines, not separately drawn approximations, so the diagram cannot show a supply response inconsistent with the demand response, or vice versa.

3. The legal battle: IEEPA and the Supreme Court

Two sets of importers, Learning Resources (an educational toy company) and V.O.S. Selections (a wine importer), sued separately in 2025, arguing that IEEPA's language authorizing the president to "regulate…importation" of property during a declared emergency does not mean the same thing as "impose tariffs." Both the U.S. Court of International Trade and, on appeal, the Federal Circuit agreed, and the Supreme Court took up the consolidated cases, hearing oral arguments in November 2025.

On February 20, 2026, Chief Justice Roberts delivered the Court's 6–3 opinion, joined in full by Justices Gorsuch and Barrett and in part by Justices Sotomayor, Kagan, and Jackson (Justices Thomas, Alito, and Kavanaugh dissented). "The Framers gave that power to Congress alone," Roberts wrote. "When Congress grants the power to impose tariffs, it does so clearly and with careful constraints. It did neither here." The ruling didn't just strike down one executive order, it eliminated IEEPA as a usable tariff tool entirely, affecting the 10% global baseline tariff, the larger country-specific "reciprocal" rates, and the fentanyl- and immigration-related tariffs on Canada, Mexico, and China alike.

What the ruling did and didn't decide

  • Did decide: IEEPA does not give the president authority to impose tariffs of any kind, closing off the fastest and broadest tool the administration had used.
  • Did not decide: whether or how importers get refunded for tariffs already paid, an issue the Court left to lower courts, where more than 1,000 "tag-along" refund suits were already pending as of spring 2026.
  • Did not affect: tariffs imposed under different, older statutes, Section 301 (unfair trade practices), Section 232 (national security), which cover steel, aluminum, copper, autos, lumber, trucks, and semiconductors, and remain fully in force.

4. What replaced the tariffs: Section 122, 301, and 232

The White House did not wait. Within hours of the ruling, on the same February 20 evening, the administration signed an executive order revoking the IEEPA tariffs and simultaneously invoked Section 122 of the 1974 Trade Act, a statute originally designed to let a president respond to balance-of-payments crises, to impose a new global 10% tariff effective February 24, 2026. Unlike IEEPA, Section 122 comes with a hard statutory clock: tariffs under it can run for a maximum of 150 days before Congress must vote to extend them, putting the expiration date at July 24, 2026.

In May 2026, a divided three-judge panel at the Court of International Trade ruled that the Section 122 tariffs also exceeded the president's authority, but limited its relief to the specific plaintiffs before it, leaving the 10% tariff in place for everyone else while the government appealed. Meanwhile, the administration opened a wave of new Section 301 investigations, one covering 60 trading partners (including the EU, Japan, and nearly all major partners) over forced-labor practices in supply chains, another covering 16 economies for industrial overcapacity, timed to produce findings before the Section 122 tariffs expire, so that longer-lasting, country-specific tariffs can take their place without missing a beat.

Tracking the effective tariff rate through 2026

The headline "average tariff rate" has moved dramatically over the past year rather than settling anywhere, which is itself part of why forecasts disagree. The Yale Budget Lab's real-time tracker shows it peaking around 16% (the highest since 1936) the day of the ruling, collapsing briefly to roughly 7.3% once IEEPA tariffs were halted, then climbing back to about 10.5–11.8% as Section 122 and other tariffs took effect through spring 2026, still among the highest sustained rates since the early 1940s. Where it lands depends entirely on whether Section 122 expires on schedule or Section 301 tariffs step in to replace it.

5. The inflation transmission mechanism

A tariff doesn't teleport straight into a store's price tag. It travels through a chain of decisions, and at every link in that chain, someone can choose to absorb the cost instead of passing it forward, which is exactly why economists have spent 2025 and 2026 arguing about timing as much as magnitude.

What's actually documented: the Peterson Institute for International Economics found pass-through to consumer prices was "modest" through most of 2025, meaning importers were absorbing the bulk of the new cost in their own margins, but projected that "by mid-2026, the delayed pass-through should be substantially complete," potentially adding roughly 50 basis points to headline inflation by mid-year. Separately, roughly 86% of the tariff burden was estimated to be landing on the U.S. side of the transaction (importers, retailers, and consumers combined) rather than being absorbed by foreign exporters cutting their own prices, as of November 2025.

6. The growth case: Goldman Sachs and the optimists

Goldman Sachs Research, led by chief U.S. economist David Mericle, enters the second half of 2026 more optimistic about growth than almost anyone else on Wall Street, and its argument is not that tariffs are harmless, it's that their drag is being overtaken by something bigger.

  • The tariff drag is fading, not disappearing. Goldman calculates that the roughly 11-percentage-point rise in the average effective tariff rate cut about 0.6 percentage points off U.S. GDP in the second half of 2025. If rates hold roughly steady from here, that drag mechanically fades from the year-over-year comparison in 2026, even without any tariff being repealed.
  • Tax policy is a bigger tailwind than tariffs are a headwind. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act's provisions, an estimated $100 billion in household tax refunds in the first half of 2026 (about 0.4% of annual disposable income) and full, immediate expensing of business equipment and plant investment, are expected to add roughly 0.4 percentage points to 2026 GDP growth on their own.
  • Business investment is expected to be the standout. "New tax incentives, easier financial conditions, and reduced policy uncertainty should boost business investment," Mericle wrote, forecasting it to be the strongest component of GDP growth in 2026, a direct rebuttal to the recession camp's claim that tariff-driven uncertainty is freezing corporate capital spending.
  • Inflation is expected to keep falling, not accelerating. Goldman's economists see core PCE inflation declining from 3.0% in 2025 to about 2.1–2.2% by December 2026 "as the impact of tariffs fades," a materially more benign inflation path than the pessimists' scenario below.

Even Goldman's own report flags where this could go wrong: "the simultaneous materialization of multiple risks, particularly a combination of an equity market sell-off and AI-driven labor market displacement with limited productivity gains, could pose more substantial growth headwinds." In other words, the growth case depends partly on things staying calm elsewhere in the economy while tariffs do their fading act.

7. The recession case: the pessimists' evidence

The counter-argument isn't a single voice; it's a cluster of research desks and independent economists pointing at different weak links in the same chain, from still-rising producer prices to a labor market that, by several measures, never really recovered from 2025's slowdown.

  • Pass-through isn't finished, it's delayed. The Peterson Institute for International Economics argues 2026 consensus inflation forecasts are "premature," projecting headline inflation could exceed 4% by year-end as tariff costs that importers have been quietly absorbing finally reach the shelf, compounded by a federal deficit PIIE estimates could exceed 7% of GDP and looser-than-appreciated monetary policy.
  • The stagflation dilemma is real, not theoretical. Stanford's Institute for Economic Policy Research points to a Federal Reserve caught between a weakening labor market (arguing for rate cuts) and tariff-driven price pressure (arguing for holding steady), a split visible in the Fed's own December 2025 vote, where three members dissented, the most since September 2019.
  • Business investment could go the other way entirely. Where Goldman sees investment as 2026's strongest component, the Yale Budget Lab's sectoral modeling shows construction output contracting 2.0% and mining declining 0.8% in the long run under current tariffs, an economist quoted by TipRanks warned falling real income could push consumer spending "into negative territory," and independent forecaster Gary Shilling has called a 2026 downturn "almost inevitable," citing frozen housing and plunging business investment specifically.
  • Producer prices are still climbing. RBC Economics' July 2026 briefing noted producer prices rose 5.5% year-over-year in June, with a persistent gap between producer and consumer goods inflation that "suggests consumer prices still face pressure in the pipeline," and flagged an incoming Fed chair reportedly more hawkish on returning inflation to target, a dynamic that could mean higher rates for longer rather than the rate relief Goldman's forecast assumes.

9. The GDP equation under two scenarios

GDP has exactly four components: consumption, investment, government spending, and net exports (C + I + G + NX). Tariffs and tax policy don't push on the total directly, they push on each of those four pieces differently, which is where the growth and recession cases actually part ways. The genuinely honest part of this chart: the net-exports effect is a real analytical quirk worth sitting with. Because GDP subtracts imports, a tariff that successfully reduces import volumes can mechanically make net exports, and therefore measured GDP, look better, even though consumers are strictly worse off paying more for less choice. Economists on both sides of this debate agree that quirk exists; they simply disagree about how large the offsetting export losses from foreign retaliation will be.

11. Winners and losers by income group

Because tariffs are a tax on goods, and lower-income households spend a much larger share of their budget on goods rather than services, the burden is structurally regressive before anyone even discusses fairness as a political value. The Yale Budget Lab's distributional analysis finds the lowest-income households bear roughly three times the burden, as a share of their income, that the highest earners do (about 2.7% of income versus 0.8%).

These household-cost estimates have moved substantially over the past year, worth flagging rather than hiding: Yale's original April 2025 estimate, made at the peak of "Liberation Day" tariff rates, put the annual household cost as high as $3,800–$4,700. As tariffs were rolled back through the Supreme Court ruling and Section 122's scheduled expiration, that estimate has fallen to the $650–940 range cited above by April 2026. The direction of that revision, down, as legal and policy developments unfolded, is itself informative about how much of the original "Liberation Day" shock has already been walked back.

Apparel and textiles illustrate the pattern most sharply: at 2025's peak tariff rates, the Yale Budget Lab projected clothing prices up 64% in the short run and textiles up 44%, categories that consume a disproportionate share of lower-income household budgets, before any substitution toward cheaper alternatives narrowed those figures toward a still-substantial 27% and 17%, respectively.

12. Why forecasters disagree: the methodology gap

It's tempting to read Goldman's 2.8% and Morgan Stanley's 15%-probability recession scenario as simply "optimist versus pessimist," but the more useful way to read them is as different bets on three specific, falsifiable questions.

  1. How fast does pass-through happen? Goldman's inflation forecast implicitly assumes most tariff cost has already worked through the system; PIIE explicitly argues the opposite, that the bulk of the pass-through is still coming in the first half of 2026. This is a testable, near-term disagreement, not a permanent ideological one.
  2. Does business investment respond to lower uncertainty or to higher costs? Full expensing under OBBBA cuts the after-tax cost of new equipment regardless of tariffs; tariffs simultaneously raise the sticker price of a lot of that same equipment if it's imported. Goldman bets the tax effect dominates; the recession camp bets the cost and uncertainty effect dominates.
  3. Is the labor market weak because of tariffs, or despite tariffs having limited effect? Goldman's own research notes AI investment "simply offset the tariff blow" in 2025 GDP figures, but flags that job growth slowed sharply and unemployment rose anyway, suggesting the labor-market weakness and the tariff story may be only loosely connected, a nuance easy to lose in the headline growth-versus-recession framing.

13. What happens next

Three dates matter more than any single forecast for how this resolves. July 24, 2026 is when Section 122 tariffs expire by statute unless Congress votes to extend them, something analysts consider unlikely given midterm-election-year sensitivity to cost-of-living politics. Around that same window, the administration's Section 301 investigations into 60 countries over forced-labor practices and 16 economies over industrial overcapacity are expected to conclude, potentially replacing the expiring 10% global tariff with narrower, country-specific rates resting on a legally sturdier foundation than IEEPA ever offered. And November 3, 2026, the midterm elections, sits downstream of all of it: whichever household-cost and inflation numbers are current in the months before that date will likely matter more to the political outcome than the underlying economic theory ever will.

Key takeaways

  1. The Supreme Court's February 2026 ruling didn't end the tariff era, it redirected it: IEEPA tariffs are gone, but Section 122, 301, and 232 tariffs mean the effective tariff rate has stayed in the 10–12% range, still historically high, since the ruling.
  2. A tariff is legally paid by the U.S. importer, not the foreign government, and the textbook welfare diagram shows why some of its cost (deadweight loss) simply disappears rather than transferring to anyone, regardless of how the growth-versus-recession debate resolves.
  3. Whether tariffs cause a recession depends heavily on timing assumptions, specifically how much of the cost has already passed through to consumers versus how much is still working its way through the pipeline in 2026.
  4. Goldman Sachs's growth case rests on tax cuts and business investment outweighing a fading tariff drag; the recession case rests on delayed inflation pass-through and a genuinely fragile labor market outweighing that same tax relief.
  5. The burden of tariffs is structurally regressive, lower-income households lose roughly three times the income share that top earners do, a fact both camps' models agree on even when they disagree about the aggregate growth number.
  6. Three concrete dates, July 24's Section 122 expiration, the pending Section 301 investigation findings, and the November 3 midterms, will likely determine the next chapter more than either forecast does on its own.
Tariff
A tax collected by the government on goods as they cross the border, paid by the importer of record.
IEEPA
The International Emergency Economic Powers Act (1977), used from 2025–2026 to impose broad tariffs before the Supreme Court ruled it does not authorize tariffs at all.
Section 122 / 301 / 232
Older, narrower trade statutes (1962 and 1974) that remain valid legal bases for tariffs after the IEEPA ruling, each with different procedural requirements and time limits.
Deadweight loss
Economic value destroyed rather than transferred, trades that would have benefited both a buyer and seller but no longer happen because of a tax or tariff.
Pass-through
The extent to which a cost increase (like a tariff) is reflected in the price a final consumer pays, as opposed to being absorbed by businesses along the supply chain.
Effective tariff rate
The average tariff actually collected as a share of total import value, distinct from any single announced tariff rate, since it blends every product and country together.
Stagflation
The simultaneous combination of high inflation and high unemployment, historically rare and difficult for central banks to address because the usual tool for one problem worsens the other.
