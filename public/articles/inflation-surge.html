The Global Inflation Surge of 2021-2023: A Complete Account

How pandemic stimulus, snarled supply chains, and a war in Europe combined to produce the fastest price increases advanced economies had seen in four decades, and the aggressive rate-hiking campaign that followed.

EconNexus Research Briefing Topic: Inflation, Central Banking, and the Cost of Living Reading time: 20 minutes

In June 2022, US inflation reached 9.1 percent, the highest reading since 1981. The United Kingdom and eurozone would soon post even higher peaks, above 10 percent, figures unseen since inflation records began in some cases. What made this episode distinctive was not just its severity but its near-simultaneous appearance across almost every advanced economy at once, following decades in which inflation above 3 or 4 percent had come to seem like a solved problem. This briefing traces the surge from its origins in pandemic-era stimulus and supply disruption, through the energy shock triggered by Russia's invasion of Ukraine, to the aggressive central bank response and the disinflation that eventually followed.

1. Executive summary

  • US annual inflation rose from around 1.4 percent in January 2021 to a peak of 9.1 percent in June 2022, the highest reading since November 1981.
  • The eurozone and United Kingdom experienced even higher peaks, with eurozone inflation reaching 10.6 percent in October 2022 and UK inflation reaching 11.1 percent the same month.
  • The surge stemmed from an unusual combination of forces arriving together: aggressive pandemic-era fiscal and monetary stimulus, a historic shift in consumer spending from services to goods, global supply chain bottlenecks, and, from early 2022, a severe energy and food price shock following Russia's invasion of Ukraine.
  • Central banks worldwide responded with the fastest coordinated interest rate hiking cycle in decades; the US Federal Reserve raised its target rate from near zero to a range of 5.25 to 5.50 percent between March 2022 and July 2023.
  • Economists initially disagreed sharply over whether the inflation was "transitory," and later over how much of it reflected excess corporate pricing power, a debate that remains only partially resolved.
  • Inflation fell substantially through 2023 and 2024 without the deep recession many economists had thought necessary to achieve such a reduction, a relatively rare "soft landing" outcome that is still being studied and debated.

2. Background: the world before the surge

For roughly three decades before 2021, inflation in advanced economies had been remarkably tame. US inflation averaged around 2.1 percent from 2017 to 2019, comfortably near the Federal Reserve's target, and central bankers in the years just before the pandemic worried more about inflation being too low, and potentially sliding into deflation, than too high. The Bank of Japan had struggled for over two decades to generate any meaningful inflation at all. This backdrop of low and stable prices, sometimes called the Great Moderation, shaped the expectations, models, and instincts of an entire generation of policymakers, which is part of why the speed and scale of the 2021 to 2022 surge caught so many forecasters by surprise.

3. The US inflation trajectory, in numbers

The US Consumer Price Index tells the story of the surge with unusual clarity. Inflation crossed 5 percent in May 2021, the first time since just before the 2008 financial crisis, driven initially by base effects and early supply bottlenecks. It kept climbing through the rest of 2021 and into 2022, reaching 7.0 percent by December 2021 and continuing upward after Russia's invasion of Ukraine in February 2022 added a severe new energy and food price shock. The peak came in June 2022, at 9.1 percent, before a sustained disinflation began.

4. How the surge compared across countries

While the United States experienced an earlier and initially sharper rise, the eurozone and United Kingdom ultimately recorded higher peaks, reflecting their far greater direct exposure to Russian energy supplies. Eurozone inflation overtook US inflation from July 2022 onward, eventually peaking at 10.6 percent in October 2022, the highest level since eurozone-wide records began in 1997. UK inflation peaked slightly higher still, at 11.1 percent, also in October 2022.

Figure 2. Quarterly headline inflation across the US, eurozone, and UK. The US led the rise in 2021, but the eurozone and UK overtook it by late 2022 due to heavier reliance on Russian energy imports, and both experienced a slower subsequent decline. Source: US Bureau of Labor Statistics, Eurostat, UK Office for National Statistics, compiled via ECB Economic Bulletin data.

Beyond the advanced economies typically compared in this way, some countries experienced vastly more extreme inflation over the same period, generally reflecting pre-existing domestic policy problems rather than the global shock itself. Turkey's inflation reached 85.5 percent in late 2022, driven substantially by the government's unorthodox insistence on cutting interest rates even as prices accelerated. Argentina and several other economies with long histories of monetary instability saw inflation exceed 100 percent over the same period.

Figure 3. Peak annual inflation rate by country in 2022. Japan's far lower peak of 4.3 percent illustrates that the global shock affected countries very differently depending on energy exposure, prior monetary credibility, and domestic policy choices; Turkey's outlier reading of 85.5 percent stemmed primarily from unorthodox domestic interest rate policy rather than the global shock itself. Source: Pew Research Center analysis of OECD data; national statistical agencies for Turkey.

5. Cause one: pandemic fiscal and monetary stimulus

The starting point for most explanations of the surge is the sheer scale of pandemic-era demand support. US fiscal stimulus alone totalled roughly 5 trillion dollars across 2020 and 2021, a substantial share of it in the form of direct cash payments to households, while the Federal Reserve held interest rates near zero and expanded its balance sheet to nearly 9 trillion dollars. Research by economists Alan Blinder and, separately, work reviewed by the Brookings Institution found that a tight labour market, captured by a historically elevated ratio of job vacancies to unemployment, was one of three key factors driving the initial rise in US inflation, alongside sharp relative price increases in specific sectors like energy and autos, and a modest rise in longer-term inflation expectations.

A notable transatlantic comparison sharpens this point. US fiscal support was delivered largely through direct payments to households, which flowed quickly into consumer spending, while European support was channelled more heavily through job retention and furlough schemes that protected employment relationships without pushing as much new cash directly into household bank accounts. This design difference is a significant part of why US inflation rose earlier and, initially, faster than eurozone inflation, even though the eurozone would eventually be hit harder by the energy shock that followed.

6. Cause two: the goods-versus-services demand shift

With many services simply unavailable during lockdowns, restaurants closed, travel curtailed, gyms shut, consumers redirected spending toward physical goods on an extraordinary scale. By 2022, US spending on durable goods stood roughly 20 percentage points above its pre-pandemic trend, while services spending had only just recovered to its earlier trend line. This divergence was far less pronounced in the European Union, helping explain why "scarcity" effects, sudden shortages driving up prices in specific goods categories, showed up more sharply in US core inflation than in the eurozone during 2021.

7. Cause three: supply chain bottlenecks

Global supply chains, optimised over decades for cost efficiency rather than resilience, proved unable to absorb this sudden shift in demand. Container shipping costs rose roughly tenfold at their peak. Semiconductor shortages, triggered when chipmakers had cut production forecasts early in the pandemic just as remote work demand for electronics and, later, vehicle production surged, constrained output across the auto and electronics industries well into 2022. Economists studying the episode found that this supply-side scarcity, concentrated in a relatively narrow set of goods categories, accounted for a disproportionate share of the initial rise in core inflation relative to its weight in the overall consumption basket.

8. Cause four: the energy shock and the war in Ukraine

Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022 added an entirely new and severe shock on top of pandemic-related pressures, particularly for Europe. Brent crude oil prices surpassed 100 dollars a barrel from March to June 2022. European wholesale natural gas prices spiked to roughly 300 euros per megawatt-hour in August 2022, more than tenfold the 2020 average, as European countries scrambled to replace Russian pipeline gas, which had supplied as much as 40 percent of the continent's gas imports before the war.

The economic impact of this shock fell unevenly. The European Central Bank estimated that the terms-of-trade shock from higher energy import costs was equivalent to a transfer of roughly 2.2 percent of eurozone GDP to energy exporters, an unusually large and sudden drain on European purchasing power that helps explain why eurozone and UK inflation ultimately exceeded the US peak despite starting from a lower base.

9. Cause five: a historically tight labour market

Alongside these supply and demand shocks, the labour market itself became extraordinarily tight. The ratio of job vacancies to unemployed workers in the United States, a standard measure of labour market tightness, rose to 2.0 in March 2022, the highest level in records dating back to 1951, compared with a historical average of around 0.6 between 1985 and 2019. This tightness pushed up wage growth, particularly for lower-paid workers, feeding into the price pressures building in labour-intensive service sectors even as goods price inflation from supply bottlenecks began to ease later in 2022.

10. The "transitory" debate

Through much of 2021, Federal Reserve officials, along with many private-sector economists, characterised the emerging inflation as "transitory," expecting it to fade once pandemic-related supply disruptions resolved. This assessment proved premature. By early 2022, even as some individual bottlenecks began easing, inflation had broadened well beyond the narrow set of goods categories initially affected, and the Fed formally retired the word "transitory" from its communications in late November 2021, marking a significant shift in its policy stance.

In hindsight, most economists agree the initial "transitory" framing underestimated two things: the persistence of the goods-services demand imbalance, and the additional shock that Russia's invasion of Ukraine would deliver just as supply bottlenecks were beginning to show tentative signs of easing. Federal Reserve chair Jerome Powell later summarised the episode as reflecting a combination of supply chain problems, a consumer shift toward goods, and a tight labour market, a retrospective assessment that broadly aligns with the academic consensus that has since formed.

11. The "greedflation" debate

A more contested strand of the debate concerns the role of corporate pricing power. Some economists and policymakers argued that businesses, facing broad cost increases and widespread media coverage of inflation, took the opportunity to expand profit margins beyond what rising costs alone would justify, a phenomenon popularly labelled "greedflation." Corporate profit margins in several advanced economies did rise notably during 2021 and 2022, and some sectoral studies found margin expansion contributing meaningfully to price increases in specific concentrated industries.

Critics of the greedflation framing counter that broad-based margin expansion is difficult to distinguish, at an aggregate level, from ordinary price-setting behaviour during a period of surging input costs and strong demand, and that firms in genuinely competitive markets should not be able to sustain elevated margins once supply constraints ease, a pattern that did broadly occur as inflation fell through 2023. This debate remains only partially resolved, with most mainstream analyses concluding that tight labour markets, supply constraints, and energy prices did the bulk of the explanatory work, while corporate pricing behaviour played a secondary, sector-specific role.

12. The central bank response

Central banks worldwide responded to the surge with the fastest and most synchronised interest rate hiking cycle in decades. The Federal Reserve raised its target rate seven times in 2022 alone, moving from a range of 0.25 to 0.50 percent in March to 4.25 to 4.50 percent by December, before further hikes brought the rate to a peak range of 5.25 to 5.50 percent by July 2023.

Figure 4. Federal funds target rate, upper bound of the range, 2022 to 2023. The pace of tightening, roughly 5 percentage points in just sixteen months, was the fastest since the Volcker-era rate hikes of the early 1980s. Source: Federal Reserve Board of Governors, FOMC statements.

The European Central Bank raised its rates for the first time in eleven years in June 2022, eventually lifting its main rate to 4 percent by September 2023, the highest level since the euro's introduction in 1999. The Bank of England pursued a similarly aggressive path, while the Bank of Japan remained a notable exception, keeping rates in negative territory until 2024 given Japan's far more muted inflation experience.

13. Timeline: the surge and the response, 2021 to 2023

May 2021
US inflation crosses 5 percent

For the first time since just before the 2008 financial crisis, US annual CPI inflation exceeds 5 percent, initially dismissed by many policymakers as a temporary, base-effect-driven reading.

November 2021
The Fed retires the word "transitory"

Federal Reserve chair Jerome Powell acknowledges that inflation has proven more persistent and broad-based than initially expected, signalling a coming shift in policy stance.

December 2021
US inflation reaches 7.0 percent

Rising gasoline, food, and housing costs push annual inflation to its highest level in nearly four decades, even before the Ukraine war shock arrives.

February 24, 2022
Russia invades Ukraine

The invasion triggers sanctions on Russian energy exports and a scramble by European countries to secure alternative gas supplies, sending energy and food prices sharply higher worldwide.

March 16, 2022
The Fed begins raising rates

The Federal Reserve delivers its first rate hike since 2018, a modest 0.25 percentage point move that would prove to be the first of many far larger increases that year.

June 2022
US inflation peaks at 9.1 percent

Energy prices rise roughly 41.6 percent year-on-year and gasoline surges more than 59 percent, driving headline CPI to its highest reading since 1981.

June to September 2022
The ECB begins hiking

The European Central Bank raises rates for the first time in eleven years in June, then again in September, as eurozone inflation climbs past 9 and then 10 percent.

October 2022
Eurozone and UK inflation peak

Eurozone HICP inflation reaches 10.6 percent and UK CPI inflation reaches 11.1 percent, both the highest readings on record for their respective series.

2023
Disinflation takes hold

US inflation falls to 3.0 percent by June and 3.4 percent by December; the Fed delivers its final hikes of the cycle by July, bringing the target rate to 5.25 to 5.50 percent, and holds rates steady as inflation continues cooling.

14. Who was hurt most

Inflation's burden fell unevenly across households. Lower-income households, who spend a larger share of their budgets on necessities such as food, energy, and housing, generally experienced a higher effective inflation rate than headline figures suggested, since these categories rose particularly sharply. At the same time, a tight labour market pushed wages for the lowest-paid workers up especially quickly: one analysis found that wage growth for the bottom 10th percentile of the US wage distribution outpaced inflation by a cumulative 5.7 percentage points between 2020 and 2022, even as inflation eroded real incomes for many households in the middle of the distribution.

Savers and holders of long-term fixed-income assets, such as bonds purchased before the surge, saw the real value of their holdings eroded by inflation running well above the low rates those assets had been priced to offer. Homeowners with fixed-rate mortgages taken out during the low interest rate years before the surge were, in effect, insulated from the rate hikes that followed, while prospective buyers faced a rapidly rising cost of borrowing that cooled housing markets in many countries through 2022 and 2023.

15. The disinflation, and why it did not require a recession

Perhaps the most closely studied and debated feature of this entire episode is how quickly inflation fell without the deep recession or sharp rise in unemployment that many mainstream economic models suggested would be necessary. Conventional Phillips curve reasoning implied that reducing inflation by several percentage points would require a substantial increase in unemployment, yet the US unemployment rate remained historically low, generally below 4 percent, throughout most of the disinflation.

Subsequent research, including work reviewed in a 2025 update by economists Laurence Ball, Daniel Leigh, and Prachi Mishra, attributes this favourable outcome to disinflation occurring largely through the unwinding of the same supply-side shocks and job-vacancy imbalances that had driven inflation up in the first place, rather than requiring demand to be crushed to bring inflation down. The ratio of job vacancies to unemployed workers fell steadily from its March 2022 peak of 2.0 to around 1.1 by February 2025, closer to its historical norm, easing wage and price pressures without a corresponding surge in job losses. This pattern, sometimes described as a "soft landing," is relatively rare historically and has prompted extensive academic reassessment of how tightly inflation and unemployment are really linked.

16. Comparing 2021-23 with other inflationary episodes

EpisodeCore mechanismHow it compares with 2021-23
The 1970s stagflation Oil price shocks combined with accommodative monetary policy and wage-price spirals produced a decade of high inflation and weak growth. The 2021-23 surge shared the energy-shock and supply-constraint elements but was resolved far more quickly, without the entrenched wage-price spiral or the need for a Volcker-style deep recession to break inflation expectations.
The Volcker disinflation, 1979-82 The Fed under chair Paul Volcker raised rates above 15 percent, deliberately triggering a severe recession to break inflation expectations. The 2022-23 Fed hiking cycle was faster in percentage-point terms over a short window but reached a far lower peak rate, and did not require anywhere near the same degree of economic pain to succeed.
Post-war reconversion inflation, 1946-48 The end of wartime price controls, combined with pent-up consumer demand and savings accumulated during rationing, produced a sharp but short-lived inflation spike. Perhaps the closest historical analogue: both episodes featured pent-up demand meeting constrained supply following an extraordinary interruption to normal economic life, followed by a relatively swift resolution.
Weimar Germany hyperinflation, 1921-23 Reparations obligations and unrestrained money printing produced runaway hyperinflation, destroying the currency's value entirely. An extreme outlier by comparison; the 2021-23 surge, even at its worst in advanced economies, remained a single-digit-to-low-double-digit percentage phenomenon rather than a currency collapse.

17. Long-run legacy

The inflation surge left significant political and economic legacies. Public frustration over the sustained rise in the cost of living contributed to a strikingly uniform pattern in elections held once inflation, though moderating, remained a live grievance: in 2024, for the first time on record, every governing party facing election in a developed country lost vote share, regardless of its ideological orientation, an outcome widely attributed to the global impact of inflation on economic sentiment.

Academically, the episode reinvigorated interest in supply-side drivers of inflation, a topic that had received comparatively little attention during the low-inflation decades that preceded it, and renewed debate over the reliability of the Phillips curve relationship between unemployment and inflation. It also left central banks more attentive to the risk of underestimating persistent, broad-based price pressures, having been widely criticised for the "transitory" characterisation that proved premature, a lesson likely to shape how the next inflationary episode, whenever it arrives, is initially assessed.

18. Key takeaways

  1. Inflation surges can have multiple simultaneous causes. No single factor, fiscal stimulus, supply chains, or the energy shock, fully explains the 2021-23 episode; it resulted from several distinct pressures arriving in close succession.
  2. Geography shapes exposure to global shocks. The eurozone and UK's heavier reliance on Russian energy explains why their inflation peaks ultimately exceeded the initially faster-rising US figure.
  3. Policy characterisations carry real communication risk. The "transitory" label, though a reasonable initial hypothesis, proved costly to central bank credibility once it did not hold, a lesson likely to make policymakers more cautious about confident early characterisations in future episodes.
  4. Disinflation does not always require a recession. The 2023-24 experience challenged long-standing assumptions about the unemployment cost of reducing inflation, at least when the initial inflation was substantially supply-driven rather than purely demand-driven.
  5. Inflation's costs are distributed unevenly. Lower-income households facing disproportionate exposure to food and energy price increases, and savers holding fixed-income assets, bore costs that differed meaningfully from those affecting wage earners in a very tight labour market.

Glossary

Consumer Price Index (CPI)
A measure of the average change over time in prices paid by consumers for a basket of goods and services, the standard US inflation gauge.
Harmonised Index of Consumer Prices (HICP)
The standardised measure of consumer price inflation used across European Union member states, enabling direct cross-country comparison.
Core inflation
Inflation excluding volatile food and energy prices, used to gauge underlying, more persistent price trends.
Transitory inflation
A characterisation, used by many central bankers in 2021, describing inflation expected to fade once temporary supply disruptions resolved; later widely acknowledged to have underestimated the persistence of price pressures.
Terms-of-trade shock
A change in the relative price of a country's imports versus its exports, such as the sharp rise in energy import costs that hit energy-importing economies hard in 2022.
Job vacancy-to-unemployment ratio (V/U)
A measure of labour market tightness comparing the number of open job vacancies to the number of unemployed workers; a high ratio indicates a tight market favouring workers.
Phillips curve
An economic model describing an inverse relationship between inflation and unemployment, historically used to estimate the unemployment cost of reducing inflation.
Soft landing
A scenario in which a central bank succeeds in reducing inflation to its target without triggering a significant recession or sharp rise in unemployment.
Greedflation
A contested term describing inflation attributed partly to businesses expanding profit margins beyond what rising costs alone would justify.
