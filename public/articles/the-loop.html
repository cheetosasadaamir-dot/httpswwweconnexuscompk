The Loop

Inside the trillion dollar machine Wall Street and Silicon Valley built to fund the AI boom, and the quiet argument over what happens if the picture underneath it turns out to be wrong.

A Longread · 40 minute read · Sources: Bloomberg, Forbes, Axios, CreditSights, Quinn Emanuel, company filings and disclosures

There is a patch of farmland in Richland Parish, Louisiana, that used to grow soybeans. It now grows a two gigawatt data center campus called Hyperion, and if you want to understand the strangest financial engineering story of 2026, this is a reasonable place to start, mostly because almost nobody has actually been there. What has been there, or at least what has been wired through there on paper, is $27.3 billion. Meta owns twenty percent of it. A joint venture nobody outside a handful of law firms and bond desks had heard of a year ago owns the rest, and that joint venture sold bonds to Pimco and BlackRock and a small army of pension funds, all of it arranged quietly by Morgan Stanley, all of it rated investment grade by Moody's, and all of it priced at a yield that, as one credit analyst dryly put it in a research note, would normally belong to a much junkier piece of paper.

Nobody involved thinks they are doing anything strange. That is, in a way, the whole point of this story. Every individual person at every individual desk, at Meta, at Blue Owl, at Nvidia, at OpenAI, at the ratings agencies, is doing something that makes complete sense given what the person next to them is doing. It is only when you step back far enough to see the whole shape of it, the way money leaves one building and travels through four or five others before landing, astonishingly, back where it started, that the thing starts to look less like an industry and more like a machine somebody built specifically to keep a number climbing.

Call that machine the loop. This is a story about how it works, who is actually holding the risk once you trace the money past the press releases, what it is already doing to a very ordinary monthly bill that has nothing to do with computers at all, and the argument now happening in low voices across trading floors and private credit committees about what happens if the assumption sitting underneath the entire structure, that the world needs an almost unimaginable amount of computing power forever, turns out to be even slightly wrong.

The Loop

Start with a single sentence that sounds almost too tidy to be true, and yet is not exaggerated in the slightest. Nvidia invests billions of dollars in OpenAI. OpenAI turns around and commits hundreds of billions of dollars to Oracle, so that Oracle can build the data centers OpenAI needs. Oracle then spends a huge share of that money buying, you guessed it, Nvidia chips, to fill those very data centers. The dollars leave Nvidia's balance sheet, take a scenic tour through two other companies, and arrive back at Nvidia with a receipt attached. Do this enough times, with enough companies, and by mid 2026 analysts tracking the pattern had put the total scale of these interlocking arrangements somewhere north of $800 billion.

It gets more tangled the closer you look, which is exactly why it deserves a picture rather than a paragraph.

Jensen Huang, Nvidia's chief executive, has heard the word circular applied to his company's dealmaking more times than he would probably like, and his answer is always some version of the same thing. The dollars Nvidia puts into these companies, he argues, are a small fraction of what those companies would have had to raise anyway, and Nvidia gets a genuine equity stake and a genuine investment return out of it, not just a sale. It's a fair point, as far as it goes. It just does not address the part that actually worries analysts, which has nothing to do with whether the deals are legal or even smart, and everything to do with what they do to the picture of demand that every other investor in the sector is relying on.

This has happened before, wearing a different suit

Anyone who covered telecoms in 1999 gets a very specific feeling reading about this stuff, the feeling of a rhyme rather than a repeat. Lucent Technologies used to lend money to the same telecom startups that were buying Lucent's networking equipment. Nortel did something similar. It worked beautifully, right up until it didn't, at which point it turned out that a meaningful share of the industry's apparent growth had been the same capital taking a lap around a small circle of companies rather than new demand walking in the door. When the music stopped, both Lucent and Nortel discovered that vendor financed customers do not pay their bills any better than customers who financed themselves, and in some cases they pay them worse, because they were often the customers who could not get financing anywhere else.

That comparison gets made constantly now, usually with a defensive shrug attached, on the grounds that this time the underlying product actually works. Fair enough. Nobody serious argues that large language models are vaporware the way some 1999 era networking startups plainly were. But the demand question is not really about whether the technology works. It is about whether enough of the world is willing to pay enough money for it to justify the spending, and on that question the jury has been out for a while, getting testier by the quarter.

There's a second, less flattering echo worth naming plainly, and it isn't about vendor financing at all. It's about where debt gets parked. Enron's collapse in 2001 is remembered, correctly, as a story about fraud, but the specific mechanism was almost boring: a web of off balance sheet special purpose entities that let Enron keep debt and risk technically separate from its own books while still controlling the underlying assets. Nobody sensible is accusing Meta or Oracle of Enron style fraud, and the structures described in this piece are disclosed, rated, and legal in a way Enron's were explicitly designed not to be. But the underlying instinct, keep the liability off your own balance sheet while keeping the upside, is the same instinct, wearing a much better tailored suit and carrying an actual credit rating. History does not repeat the crime. It repeats the shape of the temptation.

Watching the timeline unfold, month by month, is its own kind of education, because it shows how quickly a handful of individually reasonable deals compound into something much larger.

The other side of the desk

It would be a disservice to stop here without giving equal room to the argument on the other side, because it is not a fringe argument, and the people making it are not fools. Deutsche Bank's global economics team, in a mid year outlook, described 2026 as anything but dull and reaffirmed a year end S&P 500 target of 8,000, built on an expected price to earnings multiple around 25 and earnings per share growth across the index of roughly 14.2%, driven substantially by strength in technology and financial companies. Goldman Sachs raised its own forecast for 2026 US IPO proceeds to $225 billion from $160 billion in the space of a few months, and pushed its estimate of total US corporate equity issuance for the year up to around $675 billion. Bank of America's head of US equity strategy has argued that, by her team's analysis of prior bubbles, the technology sector still sits on solid ground, expecting continued double digit earnings growth even while flagging what she calls an air pocket risk somewhere ahead.

The bull case, stripped of its charts and multiples, comes down to something fairly intuitive. Advanced chips remain genuinely scarce. Building a data center takes years, not months, so any company that waits for certainty before committing capital risks arriving at the party after the good seats are gone. Locking in supply through a mix of long term purchase agreements and financing, the argument goes, is not evidence of desperation, it is simply how you compete in a market where the thing everyone wants cannot be manufactured fast enough. Asset manager Janus Henderson has described the wave of interlocking AI deals as something closer to a virtuous circle than a vicious one, a way of lining up suppliers, builders, and customers fast enough to meet demand that is, in this telling, embarrassingly real rather than manufactured.

Reasonable people can hold both of these views at once, incidentally, which is closer to what is actually happening on trading floors than the loud arguments on financial television would suggest. A survey of strategists heading into 2026 found Wall Street broadly expecting further gains in the market, even as nearly everyone quoted also wanted more downside protection than they'd bothered buying the year before. As JPMorgan's own chief global strategist for asset management told Axios, most years investing is about finding new ways to increase return, and this year looks more like it will be about finding cheap ways to reduce risk. That is not the sound of a market convinced a bubble is about to pop. It is the sound of a market that has stopped being sure it isn't.

With both sides of the argument on the table honestly, it's worth going back to the actual plumbing, because plumbing is where bubbles either get slowly defused or quietly get worse, largely unnoticed by anyone who isn't required to read a bond prospectus for a living.

Here is a question that sounds simple and turns out to have a genuinely interesting answer. If Meta wants to spend $27 billion building a data center, why doesn't it just borrow $27 billion the ordinary way, the way it borrowed $30 billion in a single, very ordinary bond sale back in October 2025?

The answer has almost nothing to do with interest rates and almost everything to do with a company's balance sheet, which is a duller sentence than the actual explanation deserves. Every dollar of debt Meta puts directly on its own books drags down the ratios that credit rating agencies watch, and dragging those ratios down for long enough eventually costs the company money on every other loan it has, in the form of a slightly worse rating and a slightly higher rate. So instead of borrowing the money itself, Meta set up a joint venture, gave it twenty percent of the equity, let outside investors, mainly Pimco and BlackRock, put in the debt and the rest of the equity, and had that joint venture build the data center. Meta will lease the finished campus back once it's built, sometime around 2029, paying rent instead of carrying debt. The data center still gets built. The GPUs still go in. The only thing that changes is which balance sheet the liability sits on, and whose credit rating has to absorb the risk.

Multiply Hyperion by every hyperscaler doing something similar, and you get a genuinely new corner of the debt markets. Hyperscalers issued around $121 billion in bonds in 2025 alone, more than four times the five year average, and total data center debt issuance across all channels nearly doubled to $182 billion. That is only the visible part. Sitting alongside it, and growing faster, is a much less visible pool of money: private credit.

Hyperion is not even the largest example, just the best documented one, because Meta's lawyers happened to be unusually willing to talk about the structure. Oracle has been running a nearly identical playbook across multiple sites simultaneously, mostly in service of its Stargate commitments to OpenAI. One arrangement, an SPV that owns Oracle's data center facility in Abilene, Texas, drew roughly $13 billion from Blue Owl and JPMorgan, $10 billion of it structured as debt. A separate $38 billion debt package is funding two more Oracle data centers in Texas and Wisconsin. A third, an $18 billion loan, is financing a site in New Mexico. None of these three deals shows up on Oracle's own balance sheet in the way a traditional construction loan would, and none of them, individually, looks alarming. Collectively, they describe an entire industry that has settled, almost without public debate, on keeping the actual debt for the actual buildings one legal layer removed from the companies whose names are on the press releases.

Who is actually holding the bag

Private credit is one of those phrases that sounds abstract right up until you realize it means a small number of enormous, lightly regulated investment firms, Blackstone, Blue Owl, Apollo, Pimco, and BlackRock chief among them, lending directly to companies instead of going through a bank. It grew explosively over the last decade doing fairly boring things, financing leveraged buyouts, funding mid sized companies that couldn't easily tap public bond markets. Then the AI buildout arrived, needing money faster than banks, constrained by their own capital rules, could comfortably provide, and private credit did what capital always does when it smells a shortage. It rushed in.

Outstanding private credit loans to AI related companies went from something close to zero to more than $200 billion in just a few years. Morgan Stanley's own analysts project private credit will supply an additional $800 billion of data center financing over the following two years on top of that. Put those numbers next to each other and you get one of the fastest expansions of lending into a single, narrow sector that anyone currently working in credit markets has ever personally witnessed.

Trace that money back far enough and it does not belong to Blackstone or Apollo at all. It belongs to schoolteachers' pension funds, to state employee retirement systems, and, in a detail that has started showing up in more critical coverage this year, to life insurance companies. Several of the largest private credit firms also own or are closely affiliated with life insurers, and those insurers have been buying up exactly this kind of debt as part of their investment portfolios. Life insurers are backstopped, in the event of failure, by state guaranty associations, meaning the ultimate, worst case backstop for a chunk of this lending chain runs disturbingly close to the public purse, not because anyone planned it that way, but because that is simply where the ownership structures happened to land.

Nobody signed up to bet their pension on whether a chatbot subscription business hits its 2029 revenue target. Nearly everyone with a pension now has.

The bill nobody voted for

Step away from bond desks and pension funds for a moment, because this story has a second half that shows up somewhere much more familiar: the electricity bill sitting on the kitchen counter. A single AI optimized server rack can draw somewhere between thirty and over a hundred kilowatts, compared with five to fifteen kilowatts for a traditional rack, and hyperscalers are installing this equipment by the thousand. Gartner projects worldwide data center power demand will climb 27% in 2026 alone, reaching 132 gigawatts, on its way to roughly 290 gigawatts by 2030. In the mid Atlantic region served by the grid operator PJM, which covers roughly sixty seven million people across thirteen states and Washington, DC, the capacity price utilities pay to secure future power, a cost that eventually flows through to ratepayers, jumped by 833% between the 2024 to 2025 delivery year and the 2025 to 2026 delivery year. That is not a typo, and it is not a ten year trend. That is one year to the next.

Residential electricity prices in the areas most affected by this buildout have already moved in ways that would have been treated as a political emergency in a calmer decade. Between March 2021 and March 2026, average residential electricity prices rose 94% in Washington, DC, 74% in Maryland, 73% in Maine, and 58% in New York, according to federal energy data, a period that overlaps almost exactly with the AI data center buildout, though it is worth being precise about causation here rather than reaching for the tidiest possible villain. Data centers are not the only reason electricity got more expensive over those five years; extreme weather, aging infrastructure, and broad inflation all did real work too. But independent monitors inside PJM itself have projected that data center demand specifically will keep pushing generation costs higher for everyone else on the same grid, and the Federal Reserve Bank of Dallas has estimated that if data center electricity demand simply doubles over the next five years, as most projections now expect, wholesale power prices in affected regions could rise by as much as fifty percent on top of whatever else is happening to the grid.

That fight has already reached the White House. In March 2026, seven of the largest AI companies, Amazon, Google, Meta, Microsoft, OpenAI, Oracle, and xAI, signed what got named the Ratepayer Protection Pledge, a White House facilitated agreement in which the companies committed to directly funding the grid infrastructure improvements their own buildouts require, rather than letting the cost land on ordinary residential bills. Whether that pledge holds up in practice, state by state, utility by utility, over years rather than headlines, is precisely the kind of thing nobody can honestly promise in advance. Senator Elizabeth Warren has been considerably less measured about it in public, at one point putting the increase for households living near the largest data centers as high as 267%, a figure that independent fact checkers found to be an outlier on the high end of a real and verifiable trend rather than a fabricated one. Texas, for its part, passed a law requiring any new electricity load over 75 megawatts to participate in demand response programs, including provisions for emergency disconnection during grid stress, which is a fairly blunt way of saying the state reserves the right to cut a data center's power before it cuts anyone's air conditioning.

Why does any of this belong in a story about circular financing and private credit? Because it is the clearest possible evidence that the AI buildout has already left the realm of an abstract financial argument and started showing up in places voters actually notice, which tends to be exactly the moment a purely financial story turns into a political one, with all the unpredictable pressure that shift usually brings.

The depreciation problem

Now for the part of this story that involves the least drama and the most actual math, which is usually a sign that it deserves more attention than it gets, not less.

When a company buys an expensive piece of equipment, accounting rules let it spread the cost of that equipment across the years the equipment is expected to be useful, rather than booking the entire expense on day one. This is called depreciation, and the number of years a company chooses, called the useful life, is mostly a judgment call. It is a judgment call with enormous consequences, because a longer useful life means a smaller expense shows up on the books each year, which means higher reported profit, all else being equal, for exactly the same pile of physical hardware.

Here is where the AI buildout gets genuinely uncomfortable. Nvidia's own product cycle turns over roughly every two to three years, with each new chip generation delivering large enough efficiency gains that operating the older chips for AI training becomes uneconomic well before the hardware physically wears out. And yet the major hyperscalers, the companies buying these chips by the hundreds of thousands, mostly depreciate them over five to six years. Microsoft stretched its schedule from four years to six. Meta adopted five and a half. Amazon, notably, moved in the opposite direction in early 2025, shortening the useful life of part of its fleet, citing the pace of change in AI hardware specifically, which makes it something of an outlier worth remembering the next time someone insists this is all just normal accounting practice.

Michael Burry, the investor who became famous for correctly betting against mortgage backed securities before the 2008 crash, put a number on the gap: roughly $176 billion of understated depreciation across the industry's major players between 2026 and 2028, which by his estimate inflates reported profits at some companies by more than twenty percent above what a shorter, arguably more realistic useful life would show. Nvidia and CoreWeave have pushed back hard, pointing to multi year customer contracts and strong resale values on older chips as evidence the longer schedules are defensible. Both sides are, in a sense, right about their own narrow claim and talking past the actual disagreement, which is not really about accounting rules at all. It's about whether the chips sitting in these data centers today will still be earning their keep in year five, or whether they'll have been quietly replaced by something better long before the depreciation schedule says they're even half used up.

The number nobody can quite agree on

Zoom all the way out and the scale of this becomes almost difficult to hold in your head properly. CreditSights projects the top five hyperscalers alone will spend around $602 billion on capital expenditure in 2026, up from $443 billion the year before and $256 billion the year before that. Other analysts, counting slightly differently, put the 2026 figure closer to $725 billion. Roughly seventy five percent of that, by most estimates, is going directly toward AI infrastructure. Amazon's capital spending this year alone reportedly exceeds the combined capital spending of the entire publicly traded US energy sector, which is the kind of sentence that should probably stop a reader mid paragraph, so take a second with it if you need to.

To fund a buildout this size, Morgan Stanley and JPMorgan's own analysts estimate the technology sector will need to issue somewhere around $1.5 trillion in new debt over the next three years. PIMCO, separately, estimates combined hyperscaler capital spending will consume roughly ninety four percent of these companies' operating cash flow by 2026 and 2027, which leaves very little room for anything to go even mildly wrong before the math starts requiring either more borrowing or a slower buildout, and slower buildouts are not exactly what these companies have been promising their investors.

Sitting underneath all of it is OpenAI, the single company whose future revenue is doing more implicit load bearing for this entire structure than any other. OpenAI has reportedly signed infrastructure commitments totaling something like $1.15 trillion across seven major vendors between 2025 and 2035, while the company itself is projected to lose around $14 billion in 2026, nearly triple its 2025 loss, against a hoped for path to $100 billion in annual revenue by 2029. That gap, between commitments already signed and revenue still very much unproven, is the actual fulcrum this whole essay has been circling. Every diagram above, the loop, the SPVs, the private credit wave, the depreciation schedules, is ultimately a bet, made by dozens of different institutions through dozens of different financial instruments, that this one company and a handful of peers will grow into commitments roughly the size of a mid sized country's entire annual economic output.

There is one more piece of this worth sitting with before moving to the harder question of what happens if it goes wrong, and it has nothing to do with debt at all. It has to do with equity, and with how much of the entire American stock market now rides on this exact same bet succeeding. A small handful of technology companies, the ones building and financing the loop described earlier, account for a share of total US stock market value that would have seemed almost fictional a decade ago. Millions of ordinary retirement accounts, invested passively in index funds that simply buy the whole market in proportion to company size, are consequently far more exposed to the fortunes of this small cluster of companies than most account holders realize, or would choose, if they sat down and picked stocks individually. A slowdown in AI spending would not stay contained to specialist credit funds and rural Louisiana bond prospectuses. It would show up directly in the quarterly statement of nearly anyone with a retirement account, whether or not they have ever typed a prompt into a chatbot in their life.

What happens if the picture is wrong

Nobody actually knows the answer to that question, including the people whose job it is to know. What people in credit markets can do, and have started doing over the last several months in research notes that sound calm on the surface and considerably less calm once you read the footnotes, is trace the sequence of what would happen if AI demand simply grows more slowly than the current buildout assumes. Not collapses. Just grows more slowly than planned, which given the numbers above is a considerably lower bar to clear than most people appreciate.

What makes 2026 different from 2008, at least so far, is where the leverage sits. The last crisis ran through banks that were themselves leveraged roughly thirty to one, borrowing short to lend long, vulnerable to a bank run in the most literal sense of the phrase. This time, a large share of the risk sits inside private credit funds and insurance companies that generally borrow less against their assets and don't face the same overnight withdrawal risk a deposit taking bank does. That is a genuine structural difference, and it is the argument regulators and bank executives reach for whenever this comparison comes up. It is also not quite the comfort it sounds like, because it swaps one kind of fragility for another: less risk of an overnight bank run, replaced by more risk that nobody outside the fund itself can see the damage clearly until it is already done.

Regulators are watching, in the specific, understated way regulators watch things before they're prepared to say so publicly. Federal Reserve officials have flagged AI related capital expenditure concentration as a topic worth monitoring in recent financial stability commentary, without yet declaring it a crisis in waiting, which is roughly the same tone regulators took toward subprime mortgages in 2006, for whatever that historical footnote is worth. The honest, unsatisfying truth is that nobody currently sitting inside the Fed, the Treasury, or any major bank's risk committee can tell you with confidence whether the private credit exposure sitting behind this buildout is more like the mortgage backed securities of 2007, dangerously interconnected and improperly priced, or more like the fiber optic cable laid during the dot com bust, genuinely overbuilt in the short run but quietly useful for the next two decades once the excess capacity got absorbed. Both stories are available. Both have serious, credentialed people telling them. That is usually what it looks like in the middle of one of these things, before anyone gets to find out which story turns out to be true.

Wall Street, to its credit, is not exactly asleep at the wheel here. Strategists surveyed by Axios heading into 2026 described the mood less as fear and more as a scramble for insurance, buying downside protection just in case, even while staying invested, because as one derivatives strategist put it, there is real career risk in being the analyst who called the top too early and missed the last leg of the rally. That single sentence probably tells you more about how bubbles actually behave in real time than any amount of retrospective analysis of 2008 or 1999 ever could. Almost nobody thinks the party is definitely over. Almost everybody is quietly buying an umbrella anyway.

So where does that leave things, honestly? Somewhere uncomfortable, and probably somewhere that stays uncomfortable for a while. The compute is real. The demand for it, at least some meaningful amount of it, is real too. What's genuinely uncertain is whether it is anywhere near $1.15 trillion worth of real, whether the chips will still be earning their keep on the schedule the accountants have assumed, and whether the small number of firms now holding the other side of that bet, the ones with your pension fund's name quietly attached to theirs, have actually priced that uncertainty in, or just decided, as an entire industry, that asking the question too loudly was bad for business.

Go back to Richland Parish for a moment, because it's worth picturing plainly rather than abstractly. Somewhere on that old soybean ground, cranes are lifting steel into place on a schedule that assumes, with total confidence, that five years from now the world will still want what Hyperion is being built to sell. Maybe it will. The historical base rate for genuinely transformative technologies eventually finding their market, given enough time, is actually not bad, and fiber laid in 1999 that looked absurdly excessive at the time carried most of the internet traffic of the following two decades. But base rates are cold comfort to the specific lender holding a specific bond on a specific building if the timeline runs long, and nobody involved in financing Hyperion, or Abilene, or any of the dozen other sites like them, gets to find out whether they picked the right decade for another few years yet.

In the meantime the cranes keep lifting, the bond desks keep pricing, the depreciation schedules keep assuming six years for chips that might realistically last three, and somewhere in Maryland or Maine or Washington, DC, a household that has never once thought about Nvidia's product cycle opens an electricity bill that has quietly, mechanically, become part of the same story. That's the thing about a loop. You don't have to be inside it to be standing on it.
