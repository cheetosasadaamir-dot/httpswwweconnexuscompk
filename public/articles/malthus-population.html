Malthus and the Principle of Population

A warning that population would always outrun the food supply, and the essay that gave economics its old nickname, the dismal science.

Econ Nexus Research Hub Briefing Topic: Population, Resources, and Growth Limits Reading time: 13 minutes

Thomas Malthus published An Essay on the Principle of Population in 1798, arguing that a hard biological limit stood in the way of endless progress: population, left unchecked, grows geometrically, while food production only grows arithmetically. This briefing works through his two growth curves, why his timing was wrong, and why the underlying logic still resurfaces in debates about resources today.

1. Background: the debate Malthus was entering

Malthus wrote partly as a reply to reformers such as William Godwin and the Marquis de Condorcet, who believed human society could be perfected through better institutions alone. Malthus wanted to show that no institutional reform could escape a biological constraint: the capacity of a population to reproduce would always tend to outpace the capacity of land to produce food.

2. The two growth curves

Malthus argued that an unchecked population doubles at a fixed rate, growing geometrically, 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 and so on across successive periods, while food production can only be increased by fixed additions as new land is brought into cultivation, growing arithmetically, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5. Because population compounds while food only accumulates, a gap must eventually open between the two, closed either by a rise in deaths or a fall in births.

Positive checks versus preventive checks

  • Positive checks raise the death rate directly: famine, disease, and war are, in Malthus's framework, nature's way of closing the gap between population and food.
  • Preventive checks lower the birth rate instead: delayed marriage and moral restraint, which Malthus regarded as the more humane, though less reliable, alternative.

4. The 1803 rewrite, and the policies it actually shaped

Most syllabi teach only the slim, anonymously published 1798 pamphlet, but Malthus spent the rest of his career revising it. The 1803 second edition, published under his own name, roughly quadrupled the length of the original into a two volume work packed with fresh demographic evidence gathered from travel accounts of North America, the South Pacific, and Scandinavia. It also softened the argument in one important way: moral restraint, meaning delayed marriage rather than misery or vice, was elevated into a genuine third check that could hold population growth in balance without resorting to death or immorality.

The book was not merely academic. It fed directly into English policy. Malthus's argument that the Poor Laws subsidized childbearing among the poor by guaranteeing relief regardless of family size helped shape the debate that led to the Poor Law Amendment Act of 1834, which sharply restricted assistance to the able bodied poor and introduced the workhouse system. Separately, Malthus's writing on the Corn Laws put him in direct, published debate with his friend David Ricardo: Malthus supported tariffs on imported grain to protect domestic farming and food security, while Ricardo argued that cheap imported corn would free up capital and labor for more productive uses. Their exchange of letters on rent, trade, and subsistence is now considered one of the founding debates of classical economics, and it happened between two men who, by most accounts, remained close friends throughout it.

5. Where Malthus went wrong

Malthus wrote before the major productivity revolutions in agriculture. Mechanization, synthetic fertilizer developed through the Haber Bosch process in the early twentieth century, and the Green Revolution's high yield crop varieties from the 1950s onward all pushed food output upward far faster than his arithmetic assumption allowed. Global population grew from around one billion in 1800 to more than eight billion today, yet famine deaths as a share of population have fallen sharply over the same period, the opposite of what a strict Malthusian trap would predict.

6. The unexpected afterlife: Darwin, Wallace, and natural selection

Malthus's most consequential reader was not an economist at all. Charles Darwin recorded reading the sixth edition of the Essay in 1838, almost by chance, "for amusement," and later credited it as the missing piece that let him formulate natural selection: if population always outstrips resources, then in nature as much as in human society there must be constant, brutal competition for survival, and any trait that gave an individual organism even a slight edge in that competition would tend to spread. Alfred Russel Wallace, working independently in the Malay Archipelago, later described arriving at the same theory through the same trigger, recalling a bout of fever during which Malthus's argument about checks on population suddenly reorganized itself in his mind into a mechanism for the origin of species.

The irony is worth sitting with: an essay written to explain persistent human poverty ended up supplying the central logical engine of evolutionary biology, a field with no direct connection to economics at all. It is one of the clearest examples in intellectual history of a single argument jumping cleanly from one discipline into another and reshaping it completely.

7. Why the argument still resurfaces

The Malthusian trap, population growth outrunning a fixed resource, reappeared in the twentieth century in works such as Paul Ehrlich's The Population Bomb, 1968, and the Club of Rome's The Limits to Growth, 1972, applied to resources beyond food, including fresh water, arable land, and fossil fuels. Modern critics of unlimited economic growth on a finite planet, including some current work on climate change and the carbon budget, draw on essentially the same structure, even where they reject Malthus's specific numbers.

8. The demographic transition: the mechanism Malthus could not have foreseen

The single biggest hole in the Malthusian model is not that food production outpaced his arithmetic assumption, real as that gap is, but that birth rates themselves fell sharply as countries got richer, which is the opposite of what his framework predicts. Malthus assumed that more food and higher wages would simply allow more children to survive, fueling further population growth. Instead, nearly every country that industrialized went through what demographers call the demographic transition: death rates fall first as sanitation, nutrition, and medicine improve, population surges temporarily, and then birth rates fall in turn as parents choose to have fewer children, invest more in each child's education, and gain access to contraception, eventually stabilizing population growth at a much lower rate than before.

This pattern, first documented systematically by demographer Warren Thompson in 1929, has now played out across Western Europe, East Asia, and increasingly Latin America and parts of South Asia, including Pakistan's own gradually slowing fertility rate as urbanization and female education have expanded. It is the strongest empirical rebuttal to strict Malthusian pessimism: rising income does not simply translate into unchecked population growth, because richer, more educated households tend to choose smaller families voluntarily, a preventive check operating through incentives rather than through the misery Malthus originally feared.

9. Key takeaways

  1. Malthus's logic, not his numbers, is the lasting contribution. Any two growth rates that compound at different speeds will eventually diverge; the question is always whether the assumed rates hold.
  2. Technology is the variable Malthus underestimated. Every major escape from a Malthusian style trap in history has come from a shift in the supply curve, not a fall in population growth.
  3. The framework transfers to other resources even where the food specific prediction failed, which is why it keeps reappearing in debates about water, land, and carbon.

Glossary

Malthusian trap
A situation where population growth persistently outpaces the growth of resources needed to sustain it.
Geometric growth
Growth by a constant multiple each period, producing compounding, exponential increases.
Arithmetic growth
Growth by a constant fixed amount each period, producing a straight line rather than a compounding curve.
Carrying capacity
The maximum population a given environment can sustain indefinitely given its available resources.
Moral restraint
Malthus's term, added prominently in the 1803 edition, for voluntary delay of marriage and childbearing as a humane preventive check on population growth.
Natural selection
Darwin and Wallace's theory that traits improving survival and reproduction in a resource-limited, competitive environment tend to spread through a population; both credited Malthus's population argument as a direct inspiration.
Demographic transition
The historical pattern, first documented by Warren Thompson in 1929, in which death rates fall first as a country develops, then birth rates fall in turn, stabilizing population growth at a lower long run rate.
