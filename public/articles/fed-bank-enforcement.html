Bank Supervision & Regulation

Is the Fed Going Easy on the Banks It Watches?

Formal enforcement actions against banks have fallen by nearly half since the pandemic — but only at the Federal Reserve. The other two federal bank regulators tell a different story, and the gap raises an uncomfortable question about supervisory rigor.

Background essay based on research by Aaron Klein and Cameron Connell Brookings Institution, July 8, 2026 Topic: Federal Reserve bank supervision

Bank supervision is one of those government functions that only makes headlines when it fails. Nobody writes news stories about the thousands of routine examinations that quietly confirm a bank's books are in order. The story only breaks when supervision was supposed to catch a problem and didn't — as it famously didn't at Silicon Valley Bank in March 2023, when examiners were reportedly still assembling an enforcement case as the bank ran out of time. That episode is part of why a new data-driven look at bank enforcement, from Aaron Klein and Cameron Connell of the Brookings Institution, deserves more attention than the dry phrase "enforcement actions" usually attracts.

The Federal Reserve's supervisory role is distinct from its more famous job of setting interest rates. Supervision means examining a bank's finances, risk management, and record-keeping closely enough to catch trouble before it becomes a crisis. Most of the time, that process ends quietly: a bank might not earn a top rating in every category, but examiners conclude its overall condition is satisfactory. When the picture is more troubling, regulators have the power to escalate — formally, through enforcement actions that compel a bank to fix specific problems on a specific timeline.

Section OneA Decline That Predates Politics

Klein and Connell's first finding complicates a tempting, tidy political story. If bank enforcement simply tracked which party controlled the White House, you would expect it to loosen under Republican administrations and tighten under Democratic ones. The actual pattern does not cooperate. Fed enforcement actions decline modestly from the later Obama years into the first Trump administration — consistent with the partisan story so far — but then the real break comes with the pandemic in 2020, and the decline continues essentially uninterrupted through the Biden administration and into the second Trump term.

In other words, the steepest and most sustained drop-off in Fed enforcement activity spans three different presidencies and two different parties. Whatever is driving it, a simple partisan account does not explain it.

❦

Section TwoTwo Competing Explanations

Broadly, there are two ways to read a decline in enforcement actions, and they point in opposite directions.

Less need for enforcement

The post-2010 Dodd-Frank regulatory framework may simply have worked: banks are taking less risk, balance sheets are healthier, and supervisors genuinely have less to escalate. Fewer enforcement actions would then be a sign of success, not neglect.

Less rigorous supervision

Alternatively, examiners may be looking less closely, moving more slowly, or facing institutional pressure to avoid formal escalation — allowing problems to build quietly until they surface as a crisis, as arguably happened at Silicon Valley Bank in 2023.

Without access to confidential bank examination records, and without the benefit of both perfect foresight and perfect hindsight, it is genuinely difficult to prove which story explains more of the decline. This is where Klein and Connell's comparative approach becomes useful: rather than trying to read minds inside the Fed, they compare the Fed's enforcement record to that of the other two agencies doing essentially the same job on a different slice of the banking system.

❦

Section ThreeA Tale of Three Regulators

The United States splits bank supervision across three federal agencies, largely according to how a bank is chartered. Nationally chartered banks and thrifts fall under the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC). State-chartered banks that are members of the Federal Reserve System fall under the Fed, along with bank holding companies more broadly. State-chartered banks that opt out of Fed membership fall under the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC).

Because the Fed and the FDIC often supervise similar kinds of institutions — generally smaller, state-chartered banks — Klein and Connell argue there is no obvious reason the two agencies' enforcement records should diverge sharply. Yet they do. The OCC and the FDIC both see enforcement actions decline in the late 2010s, though the OCC's decline is considerably smaller than the Fed's. More strikingly, both other agencies see enforcement activity rise again over the past few years — a resurgence the Fed's own numbers do not show. The Fed's nearly fifty percent drop in enforcement actions since the pandemic stands apart from both of its sister agencies.

Bank supervision has become more lenient in the past decade — and the Federal Reserve stands out for substantially laxer enforcement than its peer regulators. Paraphrased from Aaron Klein & Cameron Connell, Brookings Institution, July 2026
❦

Section FourWhy the Comparison Is the Argument

The strength of Klein and Connell's analysis is not any single number — it's the comparison. If declining Fed enforcement simply reflected healthier banks, there is no obvious reason state member banks supervised by the Fed would be behaving so differently from the state non-member banks supervised by the FDIC, given how structurally similar those two populations of banks are. Nor is there an obvious reason to think the Fed's banks are quietly resolving problems before they reach the enforcement stage at a meaningfully higher rate than banks supervised by the OCC or FDIC.

Why It Matters

Bank supervision is designed to be boring — a steady, largely invisible check that catches problems before they become depositor-facing crises. When enforcement activity at one regulator diverges this sharply from its peers, and diverges in a direction unconnected to any change in the underlying banks it oversees, the divergence itself becomes the more informative data point. It does not prove supervisory laxity, but it substantially narrows the plausible explanations that remain on the table.

The Silicon Valley Bank failure of 2023 is often cited as a cautionary tale about supervisory delay, and for good reason: examiners were reportedly aware of the bank's vulnerabilities well before the run that ended it, yet formal enforcement had not yet caught up. Klein and Connell's data suggest that episode may not have been an isolated failure of process, but a visible symptom of a broader, multi-year drift toward less formal enforcement at the Federal Reserve specifically — a drift that predates any single administration and shows no sign, in the data through 2026, of reversing.

The Three Federal Bank Regulators

OCC — supervises nationally chartered banks and federal thrifts.

Federal Reserve — supervises bank holding companies and state-chartered banks that are Fed members.

FDIC — supervises state-chartered banks that are not Fed members, and insures deposits system-wide.
