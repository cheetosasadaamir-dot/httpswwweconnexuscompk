1. Background: reconciling two rival theories

By the 1870s, economics had two competing explanations for price: the classical view that price reflects the cost of production, and the newer marginalist view, developed independently by Jevons, Menger, and Walras, that price reflects the satisfaction, or marginal utility, a buyer gets from one more unit. Marshall's Principles of Economics, 1890, argued that neither view alone was sufficient, and proposed that price and quantity are determined jointly by both forces at once.

2. The puzzle Marshall had to solve first: why diamonds cost more than water

Before Marshall could merge the classical and marginalist theories, he had to explain a puzzle that had troubled economists since Adam Smith: water is essential to life and diamonds are not, yet water is nearly free while diamonds are extremely expensive. Smith noticed the paradox but could not resolve it within a cost of production framework, since water costs almost nothing to produce.

The marginalists solved it by shifting attention from the total value of a good to the value of one additional unit. Water is abundant, so the next liter adds very little extra satisfaction to someone who already has plenty; diamonds are scarce, so the next diamond adds a great deal of extra satisfaction precisely because so few are available. Price, in this view, tracks marginal utility, the value of the next unit, not the total usefulness of the entire category. Marshall's contribution was to show that this marginal utility logic and the classical cost of production logic are not actually rivals: marginal utility governs the demand curve while cost of production governs the supply curve, and the diamond-water paradox dissolves once both curves are drawn on the same diagram.

3. The Marshallian cross

Marshall compared demand and supply to the two blades of a pair of scissors: asking which blade cuts the paper misses the point, since neither cuts alone. Demand slopes downward because each additional unit of a good satisfies less additional want than the one before it. Supply slopes upward because producing each additional unit tends to cost more, as firms draw on less efficient resources or bid up input prices. Price and quantity settle where the two curves intersect, the point where the quantity buyers want to purchase exactly equals the quantity sellers want to offer.

4. Reading the chart: the market clearing price

Figure 1. The classic supply and demand diagram. Illustrative price and quantity axes; the shape and intersection follow Marshall's original reasoning in Principles of Economics, 1890.

What shifts each curve

  • Demand shifts with income, the price of related goods, tastes, expectations, and the number of buyers.
  • Supply shifts with input costs, technology, taxes and subsidies, and the number of sellers.
  • A shift in either curve moves the equilibrium price and quantity to a new intersection point.

5. Elasticity and consumer surplus

Marshall introduced elasticity, a measure of how strongly quantity demanded or supplied responds to a change in price, essential for predicting the size of a shift's effect. He also introduced consumer surplus, the extra value buyers receive when the price they actually pay is lower than the maximum they would have been willing to pay, visualized as the area between the demand curve and the market price.

6. Two ideas Marshall gets far less credit for

Supply, demand, elasticity, and consumer surplus dominate the introductory syllabus, but two of Marshall's other contributions rarely make it into a first course, despite still doing real work in the field.

The first is quasi-rent, Marshall's term for the return earned by a piece of capital, a machine, a skill, or a patent, that is fixed in supply for the time being even though it was originally produced in response to a price signal like any other input. In the short run such an asset earns whatever the market will pay for its use, exactly like the fixed and irreproducible factor land earns pure rent in classical theory; but in the long run, because more of the capital can eventually be built, that surplus return gets competed away. The idea is essentially why software patents, prime commercial real estate, and scarce professional skills all earn outsized short run returns that inevitably shrink as competitors catch up, imitate, or retrain, and it remains a standard tool in industrial organization economics today.

The second is the distinction between internal and external economies of scale. Internal economies are cost advantages a single large firm gains purely from its own size, better machinery, bulk purchasing, and so on. External economies are cost advantages available to every firm in an industry simply because the industry itself is geographically concentrated, a shared pool of skilled local labor, specialist local suppliers, and faster diffusion of technical knowledge between neighboring firms. Marshall used the Sheffield cutlery trade and other English industrial districts as his examples; the same logic is now the standard economic explanation for why technology firms still cluster in Silicon Valley long after fast internet made physical proximity, in principle, unnecessary.

7. The Cambridge school Marshall built around himself

Marshall's influence extended well past his own writing because of who he personally trained at Cambridge. Arthur Cecil Pigou, his direct successor in the Cambridge economics chair, developed welfare economics and the modern theory of externalities directly from Marshall's framework. John Maynard Keynes, whose father Neville Keynes was Marshall's close colleague, attended Marshall's lectures and always described his own economics as growing out of, and eventually breaking from, the Marshallian tradition he was raised in. This lineage is why introductory microeconomics still runs on essentially Marshallian foundations while introductory macroeconomics runs on the Keynesian framework built partly in reaction against it: both descend from the same Cambridge department within a single generation.

One further link deserves mention because it rarely appears in supply and demand teaching specifically: the Marshall-Lerner condition, named partly for Marshall, describes when a currency devaluation actually improves a country's trade balance rather than worsening it, which depends on whether the combined price elasticities of a country's exports and imports are large enough. It is a direct descendant of Marshall's elasticity concept, applied a generation later to international exchange rates rather than to a single domestic market.

9. Why it still matters

Nearly every microeconomics essay, from the effect of a minimum wage to the impact of a new tax, is argued using shifts of Marshall's supply and demand curves. It remains the shared language examiners expect across high school and university economics courses worldwide.

10. Key takeaways

  1. Price and quantity are determined jointly by supply and demand, not by either force alone.
  2. Elasticity determines the size of a shift's impact on price versus quantity.
  3. The model describes a competitive market; monopoly, externalities, and sticky prices all require modifications examiners expect students to name.

Glossary

Equilibrium price
The price at which the quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied, with no tendency to change.
Elasticity
A measure of how responsive quantity demanded or supplied is to a change in price.
Diamond-water paradox
The puzzle that essential goods like water can be nearly free while non-essential goods like diamonds are expensive, resolved by marginal utility: price tracks the value of the next unit, not a good's total usefulness.
Consumer surplus
The difference between what consumers are willing to pay and what they actually pay.
Marginal utility
The additional satisfaction gained from consuming one more unit of a good.
Quasi-rent
Marshall's term for the surplus return earned by a temporarily fixed input, which persists in the short run but is competed away once more of that input can be produced.
External economies of scale
Cost advantages available to every firm in an industry because the industry is geographically concentrated, distinct from internal economies gained by a single firm's own size.
Marshall-Lerner condition
The requirement, derived from Marshallian elasticity, that the combined price elasticities of a country's exports and imports exceed one for a currency devaluation to improve its trade balance.
