Banking on Collapse: The Dark Side of Pakistan's Bank Profits

Pakistan's economy is being told a recovery story, rising GDP, falling inflation, praise from the IMF. Its banks are living a different story entirely: record profits, built almost exclusively on lending to the same government whose fragility created the crisis in the first place. This is how that machine actually works, with the paper trail, and precision-built charts drawn directly from that paper trail, to prove it.

Here is a sentence that should not be able to make sense, and yet, in Pakistan, it does: in 2025, the country's banks posted their most profitable year on record, Rs671 billion after tax, while the same year, unemployment remained elevated, private businesses got one of the smallest shares of bank credit in over a decade, and the government spent nearly half of everything it collected in taxes just to pay interest on what it had already borrowed, largely from those same banks. This isn't a coincidence and it isn't a mystery. It's a business model, and it has a paper trail: State Bank data, court filings, and tax records that, read together, describe a financial system that has quietly stopped financing the economy and started financing the state's own debt, at a markup, with taxpayers picking up both sides of the bill.

1. The headline nobody reconciles

Two headlines ran in Pakistani business pages within months of each other in 2025 and 2026, and almost nobody put them side by side. The first: Pakistan's banking sector was "one of the country's most profitable industries," with listed banks posting cumulative after-tax profits of Rs671 billion for 2025, an 11% increase on the year before, according to Dawn's corporate reporting and a Topline Securities research report. Return on equity across the sector hit 19.8%, with return on assets at 1.2% and a capital adequacy ratio of 20.8%, nearly double the regulatory minimum, a number most industrial companies anywhere in the world would consider extraordinary. The second: private-sector lending, the credit that actually funds factories, shops, farms, and jobs, contracted sharply in the first half of 2025, pushing the ratio of loans to deposits toward its lowest level in over a decade.

Put plainly, Pakistan's banks had their best year in memory in a year when they lent less, proportionally, to the actual economy than at almost any point in the last decade. That is only possible if the money went somewhere else. It did: into the government's own debt. By December 2025, scheduled banks' holdings of government securities had reached Rs38.25 trillion, up from Rs35.85 trillion in September and Rs30 trillion at the start of the same year, an increase of over Rs8 trillion in twelve months, faster than the private-credit market grew in the entire preceding decade.

2. How a Pakistani bank actually makes money now

Ask a banker in Karachi how the model works and, off the record, several have said versions of the same thing: why lend to a struggling textile exporter, chase collateral, provision for default, and staff a credit department to assess risk, when you can hand the same rupee to the Ministry of Finance, take a government-guaranteed 10 to 22 percent depending on the year, and never have to think about it again? By December 2024, banks' investments in government paper, treasury bills and Pakistan Investment Bonds, had reached Rs26 trillion, financing 57.6% of the central government's entire domestic debt. By December 2025, that figure had grown to Rs38.25 trillion. As of March 2026, private-sector credit accounted for just 22% of total banking assets, and by June 2026 the sector-wide investment-to-deposit ratio, the mirror image of that same imbalance, stood at 104.2%, meaning banks were, in aggregate, invested in securities worth more than their entire deposit base.

3. The great divergence: profits up, credit down

Zoom out to a decade and the picture stops looking like a bad year and starts looking like a business strategy. Government borrowing from banks has risen from 43% of all bank lending in 2010 to 73.4% by the mid-2020s, according to World Bank analysis, a structural shift, not a cyclical one. They move in almost perfect opposite directions for most of the period, only beginning to reconverge, modestly, in the most recent data.

4. The windfall: how banks made Rs110bn from a currency collapse

If one episode captures the pattern in miniature, it's what happened between 2021 and 2023, and it is worth walking through slowly, because it is a matter of court record, not allegation. The rupee, worth roughly Rs168 to the US dollar in 2020, collapsed to Rs286 by 2023 as Pakistan's foreign reserves crisis deepened. That collapse opened a wide, exploitable gap between the official interbank exchange rate and the open market rate, and banks, sitting at the center of every dollar transaction in the country, traded into that gap aggressively. The State Bank of Pakistan's own former governor put it in blunt terms afterward: banks had reaped windfall profits "not only from their foreign exchange dealings… but more importantly from the opportunity provided… to do effortless and risk-free financial intermediation."

What the record shows, plainly: this section describes a documented regulatory and legal dispute, banks challenging a tax assessment in court, which they are legally entitled to do, and ultimately losing. It is not an allegation that any individual banker broke the law. The pattern worth sitting with is structural: the same currency instability that pushed millions of Pakistani households into poverty through imported inflation was, for the banking sector, a Rs110 billion earnings opportunity.

That pattern has not stopped repeating. In 2026, the Islamabad High Court dismissed a separate petition from Meezan Bank challenging an Rs11 billion super tax assessment under a related section of the Income Tax Ordinance, upholding Parliament's authority to impose it, court records show. The same year, the Supreme Court dismissed a set of Federal Board of Revenue appeals seeking to tax corporate dividend income at 35%, this time ruling against the tax authority. Read together, the two rulings say something more precise than "banks always win" or "banks always lose": Pakistan's tax fights with its banking sector are now a permanent, ongoing feature of the system, not a one-off 2023 episode, and the outcomes cut in both directions depending on the specific legal question.

5. Who really pays the bill

Pakistan's tax system has a well-documented and almost perfectly regressive shape: the sectors easiest to tax get taxed the hardest, and the sectors with the most political and economic power, agriculture, real estate, large parts of retail, get taxed the least. Only around 2% of Pakistanis pay income tax at all. Meanwhile, in the tax year following the current IMF programme's start, the salaried class's tax payments jumped 57% in just five months, to Rs198 billion, after their effective burden was pushed toward 39%. Industry groups told Arab News in 2026 that the salaried class had contributed 352% more in taxes than several other major sectors combined.

6. The defaulters nobody names

The other half of this story is who doesn't pay banks back, and how quietly that gets resolved. Pakistan's courts have returned to this issue repeatedly since at least 2010, when Chief Justice Iftikhar Muhammad Chaudhry took suo motu notice of commercial banks writing off loans worth as much as Rs256 billion, declaring from the bench that "those who had their loans written off have built empires." A list produced in court that year named specific companies, including one tied to a sitting parliamentarian, that had written off hundreds of millions of rupees across multiple banks simultaneously.

The pattern has never fully gone away. In July 2024, the State Bank had to formally order banks to file recovery lawsuits against defaulters with outstanding loans over Rs10 million before those loans could be quietly "charged off." That same month, Pakistan's Information Commission ordered the State Bank governor to disclose a list of the country's top 1,000 loan defaulters, after the SBP had tried to claim a customer-privacy exemption to keep the list secret, an exemption the Commission ruled did not apply. One widely shared 2021 public comment on the SBP's own defaulter-reporting rules captured the frustration bluntly: the credit-reporting system, the commenter wrote, is "only applicable to masses not to the rich and powerful elite… These set of people are defaulters for billions of Rupees yet they get all the benefits and protection by the system."

In fairness: the State Bank has tightened its own rules over time, doubling the retention period for negative credit history in 2021 and ordering mandatory recovery lawsuits before write-offs in 2024. Whether those reforms are enforced with equal force against a politically connected industrialist and a small shopkeeper is precisely the question courts, journalists, and now the Information Commission have kept trying, and largely failing, to get a straight public answer to.

7. The IMF, the debt trap, and who benefits from the cycle

None of this happens in a vacuum. The current, 37-month Extended Fund Facility, approved on 25 September 2024 for roughly $7 billion, is Pakistan's 25th arrangement with the Fund since it joined in 1950, according to IMF programme records. Each programme has followed a similar script: austerity, tax hikes concentrated on the easiest-to-collect-from citizens, and high interest rates to defend the currency and control inflation, the exact interest rates that make government debt so lucrative for banks to hold in the first place. The IMF's own November 2025 Governance and Corruption Diagnostic Assessment found Pakistan's budget process still suffers from weak credibility, under-executed projects, cost overruns, and, in the Fund's own words, persistent corruption challenges requiring a 15-point reform agenda. On 8 May 2026, the IMF Executive Board completed the programme's third review alongside the second review of a parallel 28-month Resilience and Sustainability Facility, releasing a combined $1.3 billion and pushing total disbursements under both arrangements to roughly $4.8 billion. The Fund's own language on approval was notably guarded: Deputy Managing Director Nigel Clarke called for Pakistan to "maintain strong macroeconomic policies while accelerating reform efforts" amid what the Fund described as a "more challenging and highly uncertain external environment" following the outbreak of war in the Middle East.

The math there is the whole scandal in two numbers: in a single fiscal year, Pakistan's entire banking sector extended barely a quarter as much credit to every business, farm, and household in the country combined as the federal government alone borrowed from those same banks. Every rupee of that government borrowing carries a state guarantee. Every rupee lent to a struggling exporter does not.

9. The counter-argument: what banks and regulators say

A rigorous accounting of this story has to include the industry's own defense, and it is not a weak one. Bankers interviewed by The Express Tribune in 2025 pointed out, correctly, that private credit demand has genuinely collapsed alongside GDP growth, "when the economy grows, credit demand increases," as PBA figures put it, and that with growth hovering near 3%, businesses simply aren't asking to borrow at the volumes they once did. JS Bank's CEO has publicly argued the deeper problem is structural: most small businesses operate from leased premises or shared spaces with no titled collateral banks can lend against under current rules, a foreclosure and documentation framework problem, not pure unwillingness to lend. The State Bank governor himself has been the most consistent internal critic, publicly and repeatedly urging banks since 2025 to "rethink their business models," and unveiling a Vision 2028 SME-lending push with a target of Rs1.5 trillion in new SME credit.

The honest bottom line: banks are responding rationally to genuine incentives, a government paying premium, guaranteed, effortless returns, a private sector with weak demand and poor collateral, and a regulator whose exhortations so far carry no real penalty for ignoring them. That is precisely what makes this a systemic failure rather than a simple morality tale: no single actor needs to break a rule for the outcome to be this lopsided. Even in August 2026, industry reporting shows banks continuing to favour government paper over the SME lending push the regulator has been publicly requesting since 2025, a live illustration that appeals without consequences have not moved behaviour.

10. 2026 update: the rate-cut whiplash and the credit tug-of-war

Everything described so far, the government-securities addiction, the record profit, the starved private sector, is easiest to see clearly in a single snapshot. But 2026 has been anything but a single snapshot. Two things moved sharply and in ways that complicate the story without erasing it: the interest rate that makes government paper so lucrative for banks, and the tug-of-war between government and private borrowing for the same pool of deposits.

Start with the rate, because the precise sequence matters more than the headline number. The State Bank cut its policy rate from a crisis-era peak of 22% in June 2023 down to 10.5% by 15 December 2025, a cumulative 1,150-basis-point reduction across eleven MPC meetings, including a single record 250-basis-point cut on 4 November 2024 that exceeded every analyst's forecast. That decline is precisely what should, in theory, have squeezed the windfall banks earn simply for holding government debt. Then came a reversal nobody in the market had priced in: after pausing at 10.5% on 9 March 2026 as war broke out in the Middle East, disrupting fuel supplies and raising fears over the Strait of Hormuz, the SBP's Monetary Policy Committee raised the rate by a further 100 basis points on 27 April 2026, against consensus market expectations, and then held it at 11.5% through two further meetings in June and July 2026, a full percentage point above where the cutting cycle had bottomed out. Every basis point of that reversal flows straight back into bank earnings on existing and newly issued T-bills and PIBs.

The honest complication: a rate cut alone doesn't reduce what banks earn from government debt to zero, it reduces the yield, but a shrinking yield on a growing pile of holdings can still produce a growing profit, which is exactly what happened through 2025. And a rate reversal driven by an external shock, a war thousands of kilometres away disrupting oil shipping lanes, is not something Pakistani policymakers chose; it is a reminder of how exposed the entire profit model is to forces well outside Islamabad's control.

The second 2026 story is a genuine tug-of-war for the same pool of bank deposits, and unlike the rate story, it does not move in one clean direction. In the first seven months of FY26, the federal government's borrowing from commercial banks jumped nearly fivefold year-on-year, to Rs1.912 trillion, State Bank data shows, reinforcing exactly the crowding-out pattern this investigation documents; for the full fiscal year, government borrowing from banks reached Rs5.9 trillion, up from Rs5.4 trillion the year before. Yet across the same fiscal year, private-sector credit did something the headline profit numbers don't fully capture: it recovered, hitting a record Rs1.46 trillion in new lending for FY26 as a whole, a 35% jump over FY25's Rs1.08 trillion flow, and pushing the total outstanding stock of private-sector credit to Rs11.38 trillion, its strongest annual growth in four years. More than 80% of that new lending, some Rs1.18 trillion, went to actual business financing rather than consumer credit, and nearly 89% of that business lending was concentrated in three productive sectors: manufacturing took the largest share at 56% (Rs657 billion alone), followed by wholesale and retail trade at 18% and agriculture at 15%. This happened as the SBP's rate-cutting cycle lowered borrowing costs and the central bank trimmed the Cash Reserve Requirement from 6% to 5% specifically to free up lending capacity.

What this does, and doesn't, change: a 35% jump in private credit, concentrated in manufacturing and trade rather than speculative lending, is genuinely good news, and this piece would be dishonest not to say so plainly. It does not, on its own, undo the structural picture in Sections 2 and 3: private credit's record Rs1.46 trillion for the full year is still barely a quarter of what the government alone borrowed from banks in the same twelve months, and reporting from August 2026 shows bankers themselves expect no reversal of that pattern into FY27, since neither manufacturing nor exports are forecast to accelerate sharply enough to change the underlying calculus. The tilt narrowed in 2026; it did not reverse.

11. The deadline hanging over the entire model: interest-free banking by 2028

Almost none of the coverage this investigation draws on connects the profit story to a legal deadline that, on paper, requires the entire mechanism to be dismantled. In April 2022, Pakistan's Federal Shariat Court ruled that interest in any form, including the yield banks earn on government Treasury bills and Pakistan Investment Bonds, constitutes riba, prohibited under Islamic law, and gave the state five years, until the end of 2027, to convert the country's financial system to an interest-free, risk-sharing basis. That ruling has a long history: an earlier version dates to 1991, was suspended by the Supreme Court in 2002 and sent back for fresh hearing, and only returned with a binding deadline two decades later. The State Bank of Pakistan and several banks initially appealed the 2022 verdict to the Supreme Court's Shariat Appellate Bench, but withdrew that appeal in March 2023 on the Prime Minister's instruction, letting the ruling and its deadline stand.

What changed the stakes further is that this is no longer just a court ruling. Pakistan's 26th Constitutional Amendment, passed in October 2024, wrote the same deadline directly into the constitution, requiring the elimination of riba by January 1, 2028. The government has since begun visibly acting on it: a Finance Division strategy paper released in mid-2026 laid out a "Post-2027 Financial System" roadmap, and in April 2026 the state issued its first Rs109 billion hybrid Sukuk, a bond structure combining Ijarah (leasing) and Murabaha (cost-plus sale) contracts, as a Shariah-compliant substitute for the conventional T-bills and PIBs this investigation has spent nine sections describing.

Why this matters for everything above it: Sections 2 and 3 describe a business model built on banks holding conventional, interest-bearing T-bills and PIBs. The government has now committed, in its own constitution, to replacing that exact instrument with Shariah-compliant Sukuk before January 2028. The Finance Division insists existing debt contracted before end-2027 will keep being serviced on its original terms, so this is not a cliff-edge default risk. But it is a genuine structural fork: either Sukuk yields end up replicating conventional returns in substance while changing the label, in which case today's profit model continues under a new name, or the shift to genuine profit-and-loss-sharing instruments changes bank incentives in ways nobody, including the State Bank, has fully modelled yet. Watching which of those two paths Pakistan actually takes between now and 2028 may turn out to be the single most consequential unanswered question this investigation raises.

13. What real reform would require

  1. A binding, not voluntary, private-lending floor. Every SBP appeal to "prioritise SME lending" since 2025 has been publicly acknowledged, and publicly ignored, by the industry itself, a pattern industry reporting confirms was still unchanged as of August 2026; targets without consequences have not moved the ADR in over a decade.
  2. Full, standing transparency on loan write-offs. The Information Commission's 2024 order for a top-1,000 defaulter list should be a floor, not a one-off victory; recurring public disclosure would let journalists and citizens, not just the State Bank, track whether reforms are enforced evenly.
  3. A tax base that finally reaches agriculture and real estate. Every serious IMF and World Bank review of Pakistan's fiscal system flags the same two under-taxed sectors; the political cost of doing this is well known and precisely why it keeps not happening.
  4. A collateral framework that recognises how small businesses actually operate. JS Bank's own CEO has said this publicly: until inventory, receivables, and cash flow count as bankable collateral, SME lending targets will keep missing themselves regardless of interest-rate policy.
  5. Breaking the loop, not just taxing it. A windfall tax recovers some of the profit after the fact; it does not change the underlying incentive that produced it. As long as government debt outpays every private alternative with none of the risk, taxing the resulting profit is treating a symptom, not the disease.
  6. Using the 2028 transition as leverage, not just a compliance deadline. The shift to Sukuk-based government financing is happening regardless of this article's argument. The open question is whether regulators design the new instruments to genuinely share risk and return, which would blunt the "risk-free markup" dynamic this investigation documents, or simply repackage the same guaranteed-return economics under an Islamic-finance label. That design choice is being made now, ahead of January 2028, largely outside public view.

14. Key takeaways, glossary & sources

Key takeaways

  1. Pakistan's banking sector posted a record Rs671 billion after-tax profit in 2025, up 11% year-on-year, in the same period private-sector lending fell to its lowest advances-to-deposit ratio in over a decade.
  2. The mechanism is not secret or illegal: banks legally invest deposits in government securities, financing 78% of assets by early 2026, because the risk-free return on lending to the state has consistently beaten the return on lending to Pakistani businesses.
  3. When banks made a documented Rs110 billion windfall from the 2021–23 currency collapse, it took a 40% tax, a year-long court fight across two High Courts, and a Supreme Court-adjacent legal push to recover even part of it, only completed in February 2025 — and similar tax fights over bank profits are still working through the courts in 2026.
  4. Pakistan's tax system is regressive by well-documented design: the salaried class carries a 39% effective burden and payment growth far outpacing other sectors, while roughly 2% of the population pays any income tax at all.
  5. Banks themselves are heavily taxed, 54.5% of pre-tax profit, the honest complication in this story is that the pre-tax pool is so large precisely because it's funded by government interest payments, which are funded by the same overtaxed taxpayers.
  6. Reform proposals exist, from the SBP governor's own SME lending push to a Rs1.5 trillion credit target, but a decade of similar appeals has not moved the underlying numbers, because nothing currently penalises banks for ignoring them.
  7. 2026 complicates the story without erasing it: the SBP's record rate-cutting cycle reversed course with a 100-basis-point hike on 27 April 2026, holding at 11.5% through July, while private-sector credit simultaneously hit a record Rs1.46 trillion in new lending, up 35% year-on-year and concentrated in manufacturing, trade and agriculture, even as government bank borrowing grew nearly fivefold in the same fiscal year's opening months and reached Rs5.9tr for the full year.
  8. A largely under-discussed constitutional deadline, January 1, 2028, requires Pakistan to eliminate interest-based banking entirely; the first Shariah-compliant Sukuk replacing conventional government debt was issued in April 2026, raising a genuinely open question about whether this changes the underlying economics this investigation describes or simply changes their legal form.
Advances-to-deposit ratio (ADR)
The share of a bank's deposits actually lent out as loans, as opposed to invested in government securities or held in reserve; a falling ADR signals credit is going to the state, not the private economy.
Investment-to-deposit ratio (IDR)
The mirror measure of the ADR: the share of a bank's deposits invested in securities, mostly government paper. An IDR above 100%, as recorded in June 2026, means banks hold more in securities than they hold in deposits, funded partly through interbank borrowing.
Treasury bills (T-bills) & Pakistan Investment Bonds (PIBs)
Short-term and long-term government debt instruments that banks can buy instead of lending to private borrowers, carrying a state guarantee and, in Pakistan's case, historically very high yields.
Windfall tax
A tax on profit judged to result from an unusual, often unearned, economic event rather than normal business activity; Pakistan's 2023 windfall tax targeted banks' currency-speculation profits during the rupee's 2021–23 collapse.
Crowding out
An economic effect where heavy government borrowing from banks leaves less credit available, or available only at higher cost, for private businesses.
Loan write-off / charge-off
An accounting process by which a bank formally records a loan as unrecoverable; legitimate when done after genuine recovery efforts, controversial in Pakistan due to a documented history of write-offs benefiting politically connected borrowers.
IMF Extended Fund Facility (EFF)
A medium-term IMF lending programme, typically three years, tied to structural and fiscal policy conditions; Pakistan's current programme, its 25th with the Fund, was approved in September 2024 and is worth roughly $7 billion.
Policy rate
The benchmark interest rate the State Bank of Pakistan sets to guide borrowing costs economy-wide; it directly shapes the yield banks earn on the government securities described throughout this piece.
Basis point (bp)
One-hundredth of one percentage point; a 100-basis-point move equals a 1.0 percentage-point change in the policy rate, the unit used throughout Figure 5's rate-path chart.
Riba
Interest or usury, prohibited under Islamic law; Pakistan's Federal Shariat Court and, since 2024, its constitution require the country's financial system to be free of riba by January 1, 2028.
Sukuk
A Shariah-compliant bond-like instrument structured around asset ownership, leasing, or profit-and-loss sharing rather than a fixed interest payment; Pakistan's government began issuing hybrid Sukuk in 2026 as a substitute for conventional T-bills and PIBs.
