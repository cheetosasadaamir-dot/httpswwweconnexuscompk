Payments & Consumer Finance

The Check Is Nearly Dead. The Bank Transfer Quietly Won.

The Federal Reserve's latest payments study shows two very different pictures depending on whether you count dollars or transactions — and the gap between them says something important about how modern money actually moves.

Background essay based on the Federal Reserve Payments Study 2024 data, released 2026 Topic: non-cash payment trends

Imagine someone whose paycheck is deposited automatically and whose mortgage payment leaves their account automatically, every month, without a card ever being swiped or a check ever being written. That same person might still write the occasional check and pay for lunch with a credit card several times a week. Count transactions, and the credit card purchases and the odd check could easily outnumber the two big automatic transfers many times over. Count dollars, and the two automatic transfers could easily be worth more than everything else combined. That simple thought experiment is the key to reading the Federal Reserve's latest snapshot of how non-cash payments actually move through the American economy.

❦

Section OneWhat the Study Covers

The Federal Reserve Payments Study tracks core non-cash payment methods used by consumers, businesses, and governments across the country: general-purpose and private-label cards, automated clearinghouse (ACH) transfers, and checks, along with ATM cash withdrawals. The most recent release covers 2024 data. Because the study measures both the number of payments and the dollar value they represent, it offers two different lenses on the same underlying activity — and the two lenses tell noticeably different stories.

❦

Section TwoThe View by Dollar Value

Looking purely at value transferred, the twenty-five-year trend is dominated by one category: bank transfers, and in particular transfers received as credits directly to bank accounts. Their value has risen sharply over the period. Checks, by contrast, continue a long, gradual decline in total value. Perhaps the most counterintuitive finding for anyone accustomed to reaching for a card at checkout: the total dollar value of credit card payments is, in this national accounting, strikingly small relative to the value moved by bank transfers.

❦

Section ThreeThe View by Transaction Count

Flip to counting individual payments rather than dollars, and the picture inverts. Non-prepaid debit cards generate a surprisingly large share of total transaction volume — plausibly because many debit cards function as a direct, everyday draw against a bank account rather than a separate credit relationship. Credit card transaction counts have also risen substantially, especially since the pandemic reshaped consumer payment habits. Checks, meanwhile, show the clearest decline of any method in this view: the number of checks written has fallen so far that it now sits well below the number of bank transfers — a reversal of what their respective dollar values would suggest, since the average bank transfer is evidently worth vastly more than the average check.

The value of bank transfers is way up over the last 25 years — while the number of bank transfers remains comparatively low, implying a high average value per transfer. Paraphrased from the Federal Reserve Payments Study, 2024 data
❦

Section FourWhy the Two Rankings Diverge

Few, large transfers

Payroll deposits, mortgage payments, business-to-business settlements, and government disbursements tend to be automated, recurring, and large in dollar terms — but each one is a single transaction. A handful of these can move more total money than thousands of smaller payments combined.

Many, small purchases

Everyday card swipes for groceries, coffee, and gas are individually tiny but collectively enormous in number — hundreds of billions of transactions a year, each contributing only a small fraction of total dollar value.

Neither ranking is more "correct" than the other; they simply answer different questions. If the question is where does the money move, bank transfers dominate. If the question is how do people actually pay for things day to day, cards dominate. A payments system that looks completely different depending on which of those two questions you ask is, in a sense, exactly what a mature, layered financial system should look like.

❦

Section FiveThe Fee Question the Study Doesn't Answer

A Note on Scale

The Federal Reserve Payments Study is explicitly a measurement exercise — it estimates the value and volume of non-cash payments, and does not make policy recommendations. But every one of those payments involves some party being compensated, however modestly, for processing it. When the counts run into the hundreds of billions of transactions and the values run into the trillions of dollars, even fractions-of-a-cent transaction fees add up to a very large pool of money changing hands behind the scenes — a detail worth keeping in mind any time card networks, banks, or payment processors debate interchange fees and payment rails.

Reading the Two Rankings

By value — bank transfers dominate, checks decline gradually, cards are a small share of total dollars moved.

By volume — cards dominate transaction counts, checks have fallen below bank transfers, and both cards and transfers have risen since the pandemic.
